Q1. A potassium atom (neutral) has atomic number 19 and mass number 39. Which row correctly gives its numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons?
  1. 39 protons, 19 neutrons, 39 electrons
  2. 19 protons, 20 neutrons, 19 electrons
  3. 19 protons, 19 neutrons, 39 electrons
  4. 20 protons, 19 neutrons, 20 electrons
Correct answer: B
The atomic number is the number of protons (19), and a neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons (19). Neutrons = mass number - atomic number = 39 - 19 = 20.
Use after: Observe phase — Part A: Element Card Comparison (rule for calculating neutron number)
Q2. An iron atom has atomic number 26 and mass number 56. How many neutrons does it have?
  1. 30
  2. 56
  3. 26
  4. 82
Correct answer: A
Number of neutrons = mass number - atomic number = 56 - 26 = 30.
Use after: Observe phase — Part B: Rutherford Simulation (what did most of the particles do?)
Q3. In the Rutherford gold foil experiment, most alpha particles passed straight through the gold foil. What does this show about gold atoms?
  1. Alpha particles are too heavy to be affected by atoms
  2. The atom is mostly empty space
  3. The atom is a solid sphere that lets alpha particles pass through it
  4. All the mass of the atom is spread evenly through it
Correct answer: B
If most particles pass through undisturbed, most of the atom must be empty space. A solid, evenly filled sphere would block or deflect the particles.
Use after: Observe phase — Part B: Rutherford Simulation (what the bounced-back particles told the scientists)
Q4. In the gold foil experiment, a very small number of alpha particles bounced almost straight back. What did this tell scientists must exist inside the atom?
  1. Gaps in the gold foil where the atoms were missing
  2. Electrons that are heavy enough to turn the alpha particles back
  3. A tiny, dense, positively charged centre called the nucleus
  4. Solid, indivisible spheres packed tightly together
Correct answer: C
Positive alpha particles were repelled by a very small, dense, positively charged centre (the nucleus). An alpha particle is about 7,300 times heavier than an electron, so electrons cannot turn it back.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain Phase: Dalton's solid-sphere model compared with the Results Table
Q5. John Dalton pictured every atom as a tiny, solid, indivisible sphere. If gold atoms really were solid spheres packed together in a thin foil, what would you expect to happen to the alpha particles fired at the foil?
  1. Most would pass through and a few would be absorbed by electrons
  2. Most would pass straight through with no change
  3. Half would pass through and half would bounce back
  4. Most would be blocked or deflected by the solid atoms
Correct answer: D
A solid sphere leaves no empty space for particles to pass through, so most would be blocked or deflected. The simulation showed the opposite, which is why Dalton's model had to be revised.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain Phase: completing the Explanation Frame (prompt 2, deflections at large angles)
Q6. A group says the few alpha particles bounced back because they 'bounced off the electrons'. Which statement shows why this explanation is wrong?
  1. Electrons are positively charged, so they would attract the alpha particles
  2. Electrons do not exist inside gold atoms
  3. An alpha particle is about 7,300 times heavier than an electron, so electrons cannot turn it back
  4. Electrons are packed so tightly together that they form a solid wall
Correct answer: C
An alpha particle is far heavier than an electron, so an electron cannot make it rebound. Only a very small, dense, positively charged nucleus can do that.
Use after: DQB phase — Driving Question Board: 'New questions this raises' card and the 'Rutherford showed us that… but' sentence
Q7. After building the Rutherford model, your class records what the model cannot yet explain. Which of these is the question it still cannot answer?
  1. Why most particles pass straight through a thin gold foil
  2. Why the nucleus is positively charged
  3. Why atoms of different elements, such as sodium and potassium, give different flame colours
  4. Whether an atom has a small, dense centre
Correct answer: C
The Rutherford model explains the mostly empty atom and the dense nucleus, but it does not say how electrons are arranged or why that gives different flame colours. This gap leads into the next lesson.
Use after: Model Building phase — Model Phase: building the Rutherford model with bottle tops and maize seeds and noting its limits
Q8. In your bottle-top and maize-seed model of sodium, a nucleus is shown by bottle tops about 3 cm across. In a real atom the nucleus is about 100,000 times smaller than the atom. Which statement describes a limitation of the model?
  1. A real atom is much larger compared with its nucleus, so the seeds would be kilometres away from the bottle tops
  2. The nucleus should be bigger, because it takes up most of the atom
  3. The seeds should be heavier than the bottle tops, because electrons are heavier than the nucleus
  4. The electrons should sit still in neat rings, as the seeds do
Correct answer: A
If the nucleus were 3 cm across, the atom would be about 3 cm x 100,000 = 3 km across. The model also cannot show electric charge, and electrons do not sit still.
