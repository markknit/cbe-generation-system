Q1. Mwalimu Achieng writes the Arrhenius definition on the board. Which statement correctly completes it? 'An acid is a substance that ...'
  1. produces H⁺ (aq) ions when dissolved in water
  2. has a pH above 7 and turns red litmus blue
  3. contains no water particles
  4. tastes sour and feels slippery
Correct answer: A
The Arrhenius definition says an acid produces H⁺ (aq) ions in water. Sour taste is how the tongue detects H⁺, and a slippery feel belongs to bases.
Use after: Observe phase — Part A (data): data card with pH and H⁺ concentration of lemon juice, vinegar and water
Q2. A data card shows lemon juice (pH 2.0, H⁺ = 1 x 10^-2 mol/L) and vinegar (pH 2.8, H⁺ about 1.6 x 10^-3 mol/L). About how many times more H⁺ does lemon juice have than vinegar?
  1. About 0.8 times
  2. About 60 times
  3. About 6 times
  4. About 2 times
Correct answer: C
Divide the concentrations: 1 x 10^-2 ÷ 1.6 x 10^-3 = 6.25, which is about 6 times. The pH difference of 0.8 is not the same as the ratio of H⁺.
Use after: Observe phase — Part A (data): teacher prompt 'Why does a small pH change mean a big change in H⁺?'
Q3. Lemon juice has pH 2.0 and vinegar has pH 2.8. The pH values differ by only 0.8, yet lemon juice has about 6 times more H⁺. What is the best explanation?
  1. Vinegar contains more water particles, so its H⁺ ions are smaller
  2. Each change of one pH unit means a 10-fold change in H⁺ concentration, so a small pH difference means a large H⁺ difference
  3. The pH numbers and the H⁺ concentration are not related to each other
  4. The pH scale only measures how sour a substance tastes, not its H⁺
Correct answer: B
The pH scale is not a simple straight-line scale. Each pH unit is a 10-fold change in H⁺, so even 0.8 of a unit gives a big change (about 6 times).
Use after: Observe phase — Part B (simulation or paper model): beakers of lemon juice, vinegar and pure water
Q4. In a particle simulation, learners compare lemon juice, vinegar and pure water (H⁺ = 1 x 10^-7 mol/L). Which sketch of the H⁺ ions in the pure water beaker is most correct?
  1. Many H⁺ ions, as many as in lemon juice
  2. Exactly the same number of H⁺ ions as in vinegar
  3. No H⁺ ions at all, because water has no H⁺
  4. Almost no H⁺ ions, because only a tiny amount is present
Correct answer: D
Pure water has a very small H⁺ concentration (1 x 10^-7 mol/L), so 'almost none' is correct. Showing zero is a common misconception, and showing many ions is wrong because water is not an acid.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: CER task connecting the H⁺ idea to litmus and indicator evidence from Lessons 2-3
Q5. Lemon juice turns blue litmus paper red. Which particle-level reason best explains this?
  1. Lemon juice contains sugar particles that turn litmus red
  2. Lemon juice has fewer H⁺ ions than pure water
  3. Lemon juice has no water, so the litmus changes colour
  4. The acid in lemon juice releases H⁺ (aq) ions in water, and these ions cause the red colour
Correct answer: D
The litmus colour change is linked to H⁺ (aq) ions released by the acid in water. Lemon juice has far more H⁺ than pure water, which is why its pH is 2.0.
Use after: Model Building phase — Model Building: three boxes (lemon juice, vinegar, water) showing H⁺ (aq) ions
Q6. A learner draws H⁺ ions as dots to show the amounts in lemon juice (H⁺ = 1 x 10^-2 mol/L), vinegar (about 1.6 x 10^-3 mol/L) and water (1 x 10^-7 mol/L). Which drawing best matches the data?
  1. The same number of dots in lemon juice and vinegar, and none in water
  2. 3 dots in lemon juice, 3 dots in vinegar, and 3 dots in water
  3. 1 dot in lemon juice, 6 dots in vinegar, no dots in water
  4. 6 dots in lemon juice, 1 dot in vinegar, almost no dots in water
Correct answer: D
Lemon juice has about 6 times more H⁺ than vinegar, so 6 dots against 1 dot shows this. Water has almost none, so very few or no dots are shown.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: safety reminder about never tasting laboratory chemicals, and the lemon juice and vinegar food examples
Q7. During the lesson, Mwalimu Achieng says: 'We NEVER taste anything in the laboratory.' Why is this rule important when studying acids?
  1. Sourness can only be explained, not tested, because acids have no taste
  2. Every acid is harmful, even lemon juice and vinegar in food
  3. Laboratory acids can be dangerous, so we use indicators and pH values instead of tasting
  4. Taste is more accurate than indicators for finding the H⁺ concentration
Correct answer: C
Laboratory chemicals can be harmful, so we use indicators and pH data. Lemon juice and vinegar are safe in food because they are dilute and food-grade, so 'acid means harmful' is a misconception.
