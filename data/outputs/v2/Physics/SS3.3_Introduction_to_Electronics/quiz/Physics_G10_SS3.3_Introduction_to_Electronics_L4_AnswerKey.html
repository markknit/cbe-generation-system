Q1. A student connects a silicon diode (1N4007) in a circuit with two 1.5 V dry cells in series and a 3 V torch bulb. With the diode connected one way, the bulb lights. When she reverses the diode, the bulb stays off. What does this result show?
  1. The diode conducts equally well in both directions, like copper
  2. The diode lets current pass in one direction only
  3. The diode is damaged when it is reversed
  4. The bulb needs more than two cells to light when the diode is reversed
Correct answer: B
A diode conducts when forward-connected and blocks current when reverse-connected, so the bulb lights in only one direction. This is the one-way action the lesson explains with the p-n junction.
Use after: Observe phase — Part B: counter-based model grid, replacing 1 silicon counter with a 'phosphorus' counter (5 dots)
Q2. Silicon atoms have 4 outer electrons, and each one shares them with 4 neighbours. One silicon atom in a crystal is replaced by a phosphorus atom, which has 5 outer electrons. What results?
  1. The silicon becomes positively charged because phosphorus has an extra proton
  2. A hole forms, so the silicon becomes p-type
  3. One extra free electron is left over, so the silicon becomes n-type with electrons as majority carriers
  4. No electrons are left over because phosphorus forms 5 bonds with the same neighbours
Correct answer: C
Phosphorus uses 4 of its 5 outer electrons to bond with the 4 neighbours. The fifth is a free electron, giving n-type silicon with electrons as majority carriers.
Use after: Observe phase — Part B: replacing a counter with a 'boron' counter (3 dots) and noting the hole (empty circle)
Q3. A boron atom (3 outer electrons) replaces one silicon atom (4 outer electrons) in a silicon crystal. Which statement about the result is correct?
  1. The hole is a new positive particle added by the boron atom
  2. Boron adds one extra free electron, so the silicon becomes n-type
  3. One electron is missing from the bonds, a hole, so the silicon becomes p-type with holes as majority carriers
  4. The boron atom completes all 4 bonds, so nothing changes
Correct answer: C
Boron has only 3 outer electrons, so one bond is incomplete. This missing electron is the hole, and it is not a particle that was added. The silicon becomes p-type with holes as majority carriers.
Use after: Observe phase — Part B: teacher prompt 'Is the doped silicon charged overall? Count the protons and electrons.'
Q4. A piece of silicon has been doped with phosphorus so it has many extra free electrons. What is the overall electric charge of the piece?
  1. Neutral, because every phosphorus atom still has equal numbers of protons and electrons
  2. Positive, because phosphorus gives away electrons
  3. Negative, because it has extra free electrons
  4. Negative on the surface and positive inside
Correct answer: A
Each phosphorus atom has 15 protons and 15 electrons, so it is neutral. The free electron was already balanced by a proton in the nucleus, so the doped silicon is neutral overall.
Use after: Observe phase — Part B: data card 'Doping about 1 atom in 10^7 to 10^8 with phosphorus or boron'
Q5. A sample contains 50 000 000 silicon atoms. It is doped so that 1 atom in every 10 000 000 is replaced by phosphorus. How many phosphorus atoms are in the sample?
  1. 500
  2. 5
  3. 5 000 000
  4. 50
Correct answer: B
Number of dopant atoms = 50 000 000 ÷ 10 000 000 = 5. This shows how very small a doping amount is.
Use after: Explain phase — CER explanation: 'Why is doped silicon more useful in electronics than pure silicon?'
Q6. Pure silicon has a resistivity of about 2.3 x 10^3 ohm metre. Why is doped silicon more useful in electronics than pure silicon?
  1. Doping makes silicon charged, so it attracts current
  2. Doping makes silicon a perfect conductor, exactly like copper
  3. Doping makes silicon an insulator like plastic, which is safer
  4. A very small amount of dopant raises conductivity many thousands of times, and the amount added controls how much
Correct answer: D
Adding about 1 atom in 10^7 to 10^8 of phosphorus or boron raises conductivity many thousands of times. The amount of dopant lets engineers control the conductivity, which pure silicon cannot offer.
Use after: Model Building phase — Revising the class model: panel showing the p-n junction labelled 'forward-biased: bulb lit' and 'reverse-biased: bulb off'
Q7. Which description of a p-n junction diode, like the 1N4007 in the bulb test, is correct?
  1. Two n-type pieces joined together, which conduct equally in both directions
  2. A p-type piece joined to an n-type piece, which conducts only when reverse-biased
  3. A p-type piece joined to an n-type piece, which conducts when forward-biased and not when reverse-biased
  4. A block of pure silicon, which lights the bulb in both directions
Correct answer: C
A diode is p-type silicon joined to n-type silicon. Forward-biased, the bulb lights. Reverse-biased, the bulb stays off. This is the one-way action seen in the test.
