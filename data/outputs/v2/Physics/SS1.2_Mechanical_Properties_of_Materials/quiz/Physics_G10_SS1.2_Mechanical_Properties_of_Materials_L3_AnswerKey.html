Q1. A rubber band is stretched and held. Where is the energy stored while it is stretched?
  1. In the hand that is pulling it
  2. Nowhere, because the rubber band is not moving
  3. In the stretched rubber band itself, as elastic potential energy
  4. In the force being applied to it
Correct answer: C
Energy is stored in the stretched material itself as elastic potential energy. It is not in the hand or in the force, and it is released when the band springs back.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: teacher shades the triangle under the linear region of the projected force-extension graph and writes AREA = ENERGY
Q2. On a graph of force (in newtons) against extension (in metres) for a spring, what does the area under the straight-line part of the graph represent?
  1. The energy stored in the spring, in joules
  2. The safe working load, in newtons
  3. The elastic limit, in metres
  4. The spring constant k, in N per m
Correct answer: A
Newtons multiplied by metres gives joules, so the area under a force-extension graph is the energy stored. The spring constant is the gradient, not the area.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: data card calculation of the triangle area for the older rope (2 N test force, x = 0.08 m)
Q3. An older rope sample is pulled by a test force of 2 N and stretches by 0.08 m. The force-extension graph is a straight line, so the area under it is a triangle. How much energy is stored in the rope?
  1. 25 J
  2. 0.04 J
  3. 0.08 J
  4. 0.16 J
Correct answer: C
The area is a triangle, so energy = ½ × base × height = ½ × 2 N × 0.08 m = 0.08 J. Using F × x without the half gives 0.16 J, which wrongly treats the area as a rectangle.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain Task 2: Kenyan worked example A (matatu suspension spring), part (a)
Q4. A matatu suspension spring has a spring constant k = 18 000 N per m. At full load it is compressed by 0.30 m. Using Ep = ½kx², how much elastic potential energy is stored in the spring?
  1. 1 620 J
  2. 8 100 J
  3. 2 700 J
  4. 810 J
Correct answer: D
Ep = ½ × 18 000 × (0.30)² = ½ × 18 000 × 0.09 = 810 J. Forgetting to square x gives 2 700 J, and leaving out the half gives 1 620 J.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain Task 2: Kenyan worked example A, part (b) (comparing extension with the elastic limit)
Q5. A matatu suspension spring reaches its elastic limit when it is compressed by 0.35 m. At full load with 8 passengers it is compressed by 0.30 m. What is the correct conclusion?
  1. The spring is unsafe because the load is very heavy
  2. The spring has passed its elastic limit and will be permanently deformed
  3. Nothing can be concluded without knowing the mass of the matatu
  4. The spring is operating within its elastic limit, so it is safe and will recover
Correct answer: D
A compression of 0.30 m is less than the 0.35 m elastic limit. The spring is still in the region where it recovers, so it is operating safely.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain Task 3: Kenyan worked example B (bungee cord at a Nairobi adventure park)
Q6. A bungee cord at an adventure park has k = 120 N per m. At the lowest point of a jump the cord is stretched by 8.0 m. How much elastic potential energy is stored in the cord at that point?
  1. 960 J
  2. 7 680 J
  3. 480 J
  4. 3 840 J
Correct answer: D
Ep = ½ × 120 × (8.0)² = ½ × 120 × 64 = 3 840 J. Leaving out the half gives 7 680 J, and not squaring x gives 480 J.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: teacher formally introduces 'safe working load' and learners write the definition in their notebooks
Q7. Which statement correctly defines the safe working load of a material?
  1. The maximum load the material can bear while staying within its elastic limit, so it recovers afterwards
  2. The weight of the material itself
  3. The load at which the material finally snaps
  4. The load that stores the greatest possible energy, whatever happens to the material
Correct answer: A
Safe working load is the maximum load that keeps the material within its elastic limit, where Hooke's Law holds and the material recovers. The breaking load is higher, not the same thing.
Use after: Model Building phase — Model Building: annotated force-extension graph of the two Kericho ropes and the one-paragraph causal explanation
Q8. A farmer's older sisal rope stretched and then snapped when an 80 kg sack of tea leaves was placed on a footbridge. A newer rope of similar appearance survived the same load. Which explanation is best?
  1. The older rope obeyed Hooke's Law all the way to snapping, so it could not fail
  2. The older rope had a higher safe working load than the new rope, so it stored less energy
  3. The energy was stored in the sack rather than in the rope, so the rope snapped
  4. The load was beyond the older rope's safe working load, so it went past its elastic limit and was permanently deformed until it failed
Correct answer: D
The older rope had a lower elastic limit and a lower safe working load. The sack's load exceeded it, so the rope passed its elastic limit, deformed permanently and failed.
