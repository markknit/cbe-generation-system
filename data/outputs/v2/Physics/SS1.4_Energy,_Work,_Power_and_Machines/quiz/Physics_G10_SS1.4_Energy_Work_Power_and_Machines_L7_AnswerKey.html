Use after: Observe phase — Part A: friction investigation (homestead data for the 50 kg sack, rough dirt and smooth concrete)
Q1. A 50 kg sack of maize weighs 500 N. It is dragged 10 m across a rough dirt yard and then 10 m across a smooth concrete floor, both at steady speed. The friction force is 150 N on the dirt and 100 N on the concrete. Why does dragging it across the dirt need more work?
  1. The sack is dragged a longer distance on the dirt
  2. The friction force is larger on the dirt, so work = friction × distance is larger
  3. The sack gains more potential energy on the dirt
  4. The sack weighs more on the dirt than on the concrete
Correct answer: B
The weight (500 N) and the distance (10 m) are the same on both surfaces. The rough dirt gives a larger friction force (150 N against 100 N), so W = f × d is larger (1500 J against 1000 J).
Use after: Observe phase — Part A: friction investigation (computing W = Fd for the rough surface)
Q2. A 50 kg sack is dragged 10 m at steady speed across a rough dirt yard. The friction force is 150 N. How much work is done to drag the sack?
  1. 15 J
  2. 1500 J
  3. 150 J
  4. 5000 J
Correct answer: B
At steady speed the pulling force equals friction, so W = F × d = 150 N × 10 m = 1500 J.
Use after: Observe phase — Part A: friction investigation (where did the 1500 J go if the sack did not rise?)
Q3. A sack is dragged along level ground at steady speed. It does not rise, and it does not speed up. What happens to the energy used to drag it?
  1. It is stored as potential energy in the sack
  2. It is destroyed because the sack gains no height
  3. It becomes kinetic energy that makes the sack move faster
  4. It is transferred to the surroundings as heat because of friction
Correct answer: D
The sack gains no PE and no KE. The work done against friction is transferred as heat to the sack, the ground and the air. Energy is not destroyed.
Use after: Observe phase — Part B: power (stair-climb data, running 3 s)
Q4. A 50 kg learner climbs a flight of stairs by running, doing 1500 J of work in 3 s. What is the learner's power?
  1. 1500 W
  2. 4500 W
  3. 250 W
  4. 500 W
Correct answer: D
Power = work ÷ time = 1500 J ÷ 3 s = 500 W.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: exit question (runner and walker climb the same stairs)
Q5. Two learners of the same mass climb the same stairs. The walker takes 6 s and the runner takes 3 s. Which statement is correct?
  1. Both do the same work, but the runner has more power
  2. Both do the same work and have the same power
  3. The runner does more work and has more power
  4. The walker does more work because the walker takes longer
Correct answer: A
Both lift the same mass through the same height, so the work is the same (1500 J). Power is the rate of doing work (P = W/t), so the runner, who takes less time, has the greater power.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: short problem (2) on the second 20 kg bucket lifted in 40 s
Q6. Mama Kavata lifts a 20 kg bucket of water from an 8 m deep well by hand, which needs 1600 J of work. She takes 40 s instead of 20 s. What is her power?
  1. 40 W
  2. 20 W
  3. 80 W
  4. 200 W
Correct answer: A
P = W ÷ t = 1600 J ÷ 40 s = 40 W. This is half of the 80 W she has when she does the same work in 20 s.
Use after: Model Building phase — Model Building: adding 2000 J in, 1600 J to PE and 400 J to heat by friction on the Kitui bucket model
Q7. A pulley system needs 2000 J of work input to raise a bucket of water that gains 1600 J of potential energy. Where does the extra 400 J go?
  1. It is stored as extra potential energy in the water
  2. It is destroyed by the pulley
  3. It is given back to the person pulling the rope
  4. It is transferred as heat, mainly by friction in the pulleys and rope
Correct answer: D
Friction in the pulleys and rope opposes motion, so some of the input work is transferred to the surroundings as heat (2000 J − 1600 J = 400 J). Energy is never destroyed.
