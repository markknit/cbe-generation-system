Q1. On a dry Nairobi morning, Wanjiru combs her hair and her hair rises towards the comb. The comb and her hair were charged by rubbing against each other. What can you say about the charges on the comb and the hair?
  1. They have the same sign, because they were rubbed together
  2. Only the comb is charged and the hair stays neutral
  3. They have opposite signs, because the hair is attracted to the comb
  4. Neither is charged, because rubbing only makes things warm
Correct answer: C
Unlike charges attract. Friction gives the two rubbed materials equal and opposite charges, so the hair is attracted to the comb.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe, Station B (Pairs): testing each rubbed object against the polythene and perspex references
Q2. A polythene strip, known to be negatively charged, hangs on a thread. A learner brings a rubbed rod close to it. Which result is reliable proof that the rod is negatively charged?
  1. The strip is repelled by the rod
  2. The strip does not move at all
  3. The strip is attracted to the rod
  4. The strip swings towards the rod and then hangs still
Correct answer: A
Only repulsion proves the sign of charge, because like charges repel. Attraction could also happen with a neutral object or an oppositely charged one.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: comparing the class order with the triboelectric table (glass with silk pair)
Q3. This is the triboelectric order from most positive to most negative: dry human hair (+), glass, perspex, wool/fur, silk, polythene, ebonite, latex balloon (-). A glass rod is rubbed with a piece of silk. What charges do they gain?
  1. Glass becomes positive and silk becomes negative
  2. Glass becomes negative and silk becomes positive
  3. Both stay neutral because they are different materials
  4. Both become positive
Correct answer: A
The material higher in the list becomes positive when rubbed with one lower in the list. Glass is higher than silk, so glass loses electrons and becomes positive, and silk gains them and becomes negative.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain, Steps 1-2: particle-level diagram of electron transfer between hair and balloon
Q4. A balloon is rubbed on dry hair. The balloon becomes negative and the hair becomes positive. What happened during the rubbing?
  1. Positive charges moved from the balloon to the hair
  2. Electrons moved from the hair to the balloon
  3. Electrons were created on the balloon by the rubbing
  4. Protons moved from the balloon to the hair
Correct answer: B
In a solid only electrons move, because protons are fixed in the nucleus. The balloon gains extra electrons and is negative, and the hair has a deficit of electrons and is positive.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain, Step 3: charge conservation (6 electrons moved: balloon -6, hair +6, total 0)
Q5. A balloon is rubbed on dry hair and gains 6 × 10^9 extra electrons. What is true about the hair and the total charge?
  1. The hair loses 6 × 10^9 electrons, so the hair is positive and the total charge stays zero
  2. The hair also gains 6 × 10^9 electrons, so both become negative
  3. The hair loses 6 × 10^9 protons, so the hair is positive
  4. The hair stays neutral, because the balloon made its own charge
Correct answer: A
Charge is transferred, not created. The electrons the balloon gains come from the hair, so the hair has an equal and opposite charge and the total charge stays zero.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain, Step 4: calculation of the number of electrons for 1 nC, then 2 nC
Q6. A rubbed balloon gains a charge of 2 nC (2 × 10^-9 C). One electron has a charge of 1.6 × 10^-19 C. About how many extra electrons did the balloon gain?
  1. 1.25 × 10^9
  2. 3.2 × 10^-28
  3. 8 × 10^-11
  4. 1.25 × 10^10
Correct answer: D
Number of electrons = total charge ÷ charge of one electron = 2 × 10^-9 ÷ 1.6 × 10^-19 = 1.25 × 10^10. This is still a tiny fraction of the roughly 10^23 atoms in a balloon.
Use after: DQB phase — DQB: new question 'Why does the balloon stick to the neutral wall?'
Q7. A balloon rubbed on hair sticks to a classroom wall. The wall was never rubbed. Why can rubbing alone NOT explain why the balloon sticks to the wall?
  1. The wall was not rubbed, so a different explanation is needed for its attraction
  2. Friction between balloon and hair made the wall positive
  3. The wall has protons that move towards the balloon
  4. The wall gained electrons from the balloon by friction
Correct answer: A
Charging by friction explains why the balloon and hair become charged. The wall was not rubbed, so its attraction to the balloon needs another explanation, which the class will meet in a later lesson.
