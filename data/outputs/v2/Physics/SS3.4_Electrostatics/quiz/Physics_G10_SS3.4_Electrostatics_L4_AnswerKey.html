Q1. In a circuit with two 1.5 V cells in series (3 V) and a torch bulb, a learner touches each material to the gap in turn. Which list contains ONLY materials that made the bulb light?
  1. Aluminium foil, dry wood, glass rod, graphite
  2. Copper wire, aluminium foil, steel spoon, graphite
  3. Steel spoon, rubber, copper wire, dry cotton cloth
  4. Copper wire, plastic ruler, steel spoon, graphite
Correct answer: B
Copper, aluminium, steel and graphite are conductors, so the bulb lights. Plastic, dry wood, rubber, glass and dry cotton are insulators, so the bulb stays off.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: teacher asks 'Is the circuit broken, or is the material not letting electrons pass?' and groups test the bulb first
Q2. A group touches the two bare wire ends of their circuit together and the bulb lights. They then place a dry wooden stick across the gap and the bulb does NOT light. What is the best conclusion?
  1. The cell has gone flat
  2. Dry wood is an insulator, so electrons cannot move through it
  3. Wood has no electrons in it
  4. The circuit is broken, so nothing can be learned about wood
Correct answer: B
The bulb lit when the wires touched, so the circuit and cell work. The bulb stayed off with wood because its electrons are tightly bound and cannot move through it. Wood does have electrons.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: prompt 'Which result surprised your group? Is graphite a metal?'
Q3. Graphite (pencil lead) is not a metal, yet the bulb lights when graphite is placed in the gap of the circuit. What does this result tell us?
  1. Pencil lead is secretly a metal
  2. Graphite has free electrons that can move, so metals are not the only conductors
  3. The bulb was lit by the wood around the pencil lead
  4. Graphite has free protons that carry the electricity
Correct answer: B
The bulb lit, so graphite has free (delocalised) electrons. This shows that the rule 'only metals conduct' is not enough, and we need the idea of free electrons.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: sorting the 9 materials and drawing free versus bound electrons in a conductor and an insulator
Q4. What is different about the electrons in a copper wire and in a plastic ruler?
  1. Copper has free electrons that can move through it, but plastic's electrons are tightly bound to their atoms
  2. Copper has electrons but plastic has none
  3. Copper's electrons are positive and plastic's are negative
  4. Plastic has more electrons, so they cannot move
Correct answer: A
Both materials contain electrons. In copper some are free (delocalised) and drift through the metal, but in plastic they are tightly bound and cannot move through the material.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: teacher says 'Only electrons move in solids; protons stay in the nucleus' and asks why we say free electrons, not free protons
Q5. Why do we say a conductor has free electrons and not free protons?
  1. Protons are too small to move
  2. In solids the protons stay fixed in the nucleus, so only electrons can move
  3. Protons are free in metals, but electrons are free in insulators
  4. Protons are negatively charged, so they are repelled by the cell
Correct answer: B
Protons are held in the nucleus of each atom, so in a solid only electrons move. Protons are positively charged, not negatively charged.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: applying the idea to the balloon ('Why does the extra charge on the rubbed balloon stay on the balloon?')
Q6. A latex balloon is rubbed on hair and gains extra electrons. It then stays charged and stuck to a wall for about 3 minutes on a dry day. Which explanation is correct?
  1. The wall keeps giving the balloon new electrons all the time
  2. Latex is an insulator, so the extra electrons cannot move through it and stay where they were deposited
  3. Latex is a conductor, so the electrons stay trapped inside the balloon
  4. The electrons are used up as soon as the balloon touches the wall, so the charge must be made again
Correct answer: B
Latex rubber is an insulator, as the bulb test showed. Its electrons are bound, so the extra electrons cannot spread through the balloon and the charge stays in place.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: groups compare results with their predictions and retest any 'odd' result once
Q7. A group predicted that a glass rod would light the bulb, but the bulb stayed off. The group retested it once with firm contacts and got the same result. What should they do next?
  1. Record 'Not lit' honestly and mark glass as an insulator
  2. Leave the result blank because it was a surprise
  3. Record 'Lit' because glass is a solid like metals
  4. Change the recorded result to 'Lit' so it matches their prediction
Correct answer: A
Good investigators record what they observe, even when it surprises them. The retest confirmed the result, so glass is recorded as not lit and classed as an insulator.
Use after: Model Building phase — Model: learners revise their particle-level model of the balloon, hair, wall and paper in a different colour
Q8. A balloon is rubbed on hair, and electrons move from the hair to the balloon. Which description should be in the revised particle model?
  1. Both the balloon and the hair are neutral because charge is destroyed by rubbing
  2. The balloon is negative because it gained electrons, and the hair is positive because it lost electrons
  3. The balloon is positive because it gained protons, and the hair is negative
  4. The balloon is negative because positive charges moved from the balloon to the hair
Correct answer: B
Only electrons move in solids. The balloon gains electrons and becomes negative, and the hair loses electrons and becomes positive. Charge is moved, not created or destroyed.
