Q1. The boiling points of three Group VI hydrides are: H₂Te −2 °C, H₂Se −41 °C, H₂S −60 °C. The boiling point falls as the molecules get smaller. If water (H₂O, the smallest of these molecules) followed this same trend, what boiling point would you expect?
  1. About −41 °C, the same as H₂Se
  2. Lower than −60 °C, roughly −80 °C
  3. About 0 °C, the freezing point of water
  4. About 100 °C, because water is a very common liquid
Correct answer: B
The trend shows boiling point dropping as molecules get smaller, so extending it to the smallest molecule (H₂O) gives a value below −60 °C. The real value of 100 °C is an anomaly, which is the puzzle for the lesson.
Use after: Observe phase — Activity A: Iodine Sublimation Demonstration
Q2. A few iodine crystals in a flask are gently warmed in a water bath at about 50 °C. Purple vapour forms directly from the solid, with almost no liquid seen. What is this change of state called?
  1. Melting followed by boiling
  2. Evaporation of a liquid
  3. Sublimation
  4. Condensation
Correct answer: C
A solid changing directly into a gas without passing through the liquid state is sublimation. Saying iodine 'melted' is the red flag the teacher looks for.
Use after: Observe phase — Activity B: Boiling Point Data Analysis (comparing iodine with xenon)
Q3. Xenon (Xe) atoms have 54 electrons and the substance boils at −108 °C. Iodine (I₂) molecules have 106 electrons and the substance boils at 184 °C. Neither substance can form hydrogen bonds. What best explains why iodine boils so much higher than xenon?
  1. The larger electron cloud of I₂ is easier to distort, so its London dispersion forces are stronger
  2. The covalent bond inside I₂ is stronger than anything holding Xe atoms together, so more energy is needed to boil I₂
  3. Xenon atoms attract one another more strongly because they are single atoms
  4. I₂ forms hydrogen bonds between its molecules
Correct answer: A
More electrons mean a larger electron cloud that is more easily distorted into temporary dipoles. This gives stronger London dispersion forces between I₂ molecules, so more energy is needed to separate them. Boiling does not break the covalent bond inside I₂.
Use after: Explain phase — Part A: Hydrogen Bonding (rule: H bonded to N, O or F; comparing water with H₂S)
Q4. Hydrogen bonding can form between molecules when hydrogen is covalently bonded to a very electronegative atom. Which of these substances has molecules that can form hydrogen bonds with each other?
  1. Argon (Ar)
  2. Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S)
  3. Water (H₂O)
  4. Hydrogen chloride (HCl)
Correct answer: C
Hydrogen bonding needs H bonded to N, O or F. Only water has H bonded to one of these (oxygen). Sulfur and chlorine are not electronegative enough in the way the lesson rule requires, and argon has no hydrogen.
Use after: Explain phase — Part A: Hydrogen Bonding (diagram of three water molecules, and the exit mini-task of drawing one hydrogen bond)
Q5. Two water molecules are drawn next to each other. Where does the hydrogen bond between them form?
  1. Between the two hydrogen atoms of the same molecule, shown as a solid line
  2. Between a hydrogen atom of one molecule and a hydrogen atom of the other molecule
  3. Between the two oxygen atoms, one from each molecule
  4. Between the δ+ hydrogen of one molecule and a lone pair on the δ− oxygen of the neighbouring molecule, shown as a dashed line
Correct answer: D
A hydrogen bond is a weak electrostatic attraction between the δ+ H of one molecule and a lone pair on the δ− O of a neighbouring molecule. It is drawn as a dashed line, not a solid covalent bond.
Use after: DQB phase — Driving Question Board: green sticky note connecting intermolecular forces to graphite
Q6. Graphite has flat layers of carbon atoms. Within each layer the atoms are joined by strong covalent bonds. Why can the layers slide over each other so that graphite is soft and leaves a mark on paper?
  1. The covalent bonds between the layers are weak because carbon atoms are large
  2. Only weak Van der Waals forces hold the layers together, and these are much weaker than the covalent bonds within the layers
  3. The layers are held together by strong hydrogen bonds that let them move
  4. The covalent bonds inside each layer break easily when you press on graphite
Correct answer: B
Between the layers there are only weak Van der Waals forces, so the layers slide easily. Diamond has no such weak links because its 3-D network is covalently bonded throughout.
Use after: Model Building phase — Cumulative particle model of carbon structures: revising the graphite portion
Q7. You are drawing a particle model of graphite. Which drawing convention correctly shows the forces inside and between the layers?
  1. Dashed lines between carbon atoms within each layer, and solid lines between the layers
  2. Solid lines for covalent bonds within each hexagonal layer, and dashed lines between the layers labelled weak Van der Waals forces
  3. Solid lines between the layers, labelled as strong covalent bonds, to show that the layers are firmly joined
  4. Hydrogen bonds drawn as dashed lines between the layers
Correct answer: B
Strong covalent bonds inside each layer are drawn as solid lines. The weak Van der Waals forces between the layers are drawn as dashed lines. Mixing these up confuses forces within layers with forces between layers.
