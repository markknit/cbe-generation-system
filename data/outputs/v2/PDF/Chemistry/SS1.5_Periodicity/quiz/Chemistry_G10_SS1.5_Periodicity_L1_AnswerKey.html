Q1. Before the demonstration, a student writes: "Sodium will be safe in Lake Victoria water because we eat it every day in salt on our ugali." What is the main problem with this prediction?
  1. Sodium metal is only found in the sea, so it cannot be tested with lake water.
  2. Salt is sodium chloride, a compound. Pure sodium metal is a different substance and can behave very differently.
  3. Sodium is a gas, so it would escape from the water before reacting.
  4. Sodium in salt and pure sodium metal are exactly the same, so the prediction is correct but badly explained.
Correct answer: B
The common misconception is to confuse the compound (sodium chloride) with the element (sodium metal). The two are different substances with different properties.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: live demonstration or video of sodium, magnesium, aluminium and argon (Observation Table)
Q2. In the demonstration, each substance was placed in cold water. Which row correctly describes what was observed?
  1. Sodium, magnesium and aluminium: all fizz in the same way. Argon: no change.
  2. Sodium: barely fizzes. Magnesium: skims and burns with an orange flame. Aluminium: fizzes slowly. Argon: dissolves.
  3. Sodium: skims and fizzes with an orange flame. Magnesium: barely fizzes. Aluminium: no visible reaction. Argon: no change.
  4. Sodium: no reaction. Magnesium: violent fizzing. Aluminium: a few bubbles. Argon: orange flame.
Correct answer: C
Sodium reacts violently, magnesium shows only a few tiny bubbles, aluminium shows no visible change and argon shows no change. This tests the left-to-right pattern across Period 3 that the students saw.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: sodium dropped into water, solution turns phenolphthalein pink
Q3. A drop of phenolphthalein indicator was added to the water after sodium reacted with it. The solution turned pink. What can students conclude from this observation?
  1. The solution has become acidic.
  2. The solution is still neutral, the same as the original water.
  3. The solution has become alkaline.
  4. The solution has become very hot, and heat turns phenolphthalein pink.
Correct answer: C
Phenolphthalein turns pink in alkaline solutions. The lesson says sodium leaves behind a strongly alkaline solution. Why this happens is not explained yet.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: structured Observation Table (What I See | What I Hear | What I Infer)
Q4. Students filled in an Observation Table while watching sodium react with water. Which entry belongs in the "What I Infer" column, because it is an inference and not a direct observation?
  1. The flame was orange.
  2. A gas is being produced.
  3. The solution turned pink after the indicator was added.
  4. The sodium skimmed across the surface of the water.
Correct answer: B
Skimming, flame colour and the pink colour can all be seen directly. A gas being made is a guess based on the fizzing, so it is an inference.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: Group I Physical Properties data sheet, question 3 (lithium in the chef's pot)
Q5. In the class demonstration, potassium reacted with water more violently than sodium, and sodium reacted more violently than lithium. A chef in Mombasa drops a small piece of lithium into a pot of water. Based on this pattern in Group I, how will the reaction compare with the sodium reaction the class watched?
  1. It will be more violent, because lithium is higher up the group.
  2. It will be less violent, because reactivity increases going down Group I and lithium is above sodium.
  3. It will be more violent, because lithium is lighter than sodium.
  4. It will be exactly the same, because lithium and sodium are in the same group.
Correct answer: B
Reactivity with water increases down Group I (Li < Na < K). Lithium sits above sodium, so it reacts less violently. The option about "lighter" reflects the misconception named in the lesson.
Use after: DQB phase — DQB: Driving Question Board (YELLOW notes: what I want to know to understand the sodium explosion)
Q6. Sodium and magnesium are neighbours in Period 3, but sodium reacts violently with cold water and magnesium barely reacts. At the end of Lesson 1, which statement about WHY they differ is most accurate?
  1. Both are metals, so they should react in the same way and the difference must be a mistake.
  2. Sodium reacts more violently because it is found in salt.
  3. The class has only observed the difference so far. The reason is still an open question to investigate in later lessons.
  4. Magnesium is not a metal, so it cannot react like sodium.
Correct answer: C
This is the anchor lesson. Students record the puzzle on the Driving Question Board and have not yet been taught a reason. Magnesium is a metal, and the salt idea is a misconception.
Use after: End of lesson — Observe: argon ampoule photograph or video (end-of-lesson recap of Period 3 pattern)
Q7. Argon sits right next to chlorine on the periodic table. When the class was told what happens if argon is tapped, shaken or has a flame held near it, what was the result?
  1. It reacts slowly with a few bubbles, like magnesium.
  2. It reacts like chlorine because they are neighbours.
  3. It burns with an orange flame, like sodium.
  4. Nothing happens. Argon shows no change at all.
Correct answer: D
Argon is completely inert and shows no change. Being next to a reactive element does not make an element reactive, so the pattern reverses at the noble gases.
