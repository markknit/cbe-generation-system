Q1. A learner says, "Copper sulphate is a coloured solid, so it will not dissolve in water." Which is the best way to test this idea?
  1. Look at the colour of the solid again and decide
  2. Keep the sample in the vial and compare it with the white salts
  3. Add a small measured amount to 5 mL of water in a test tube, stir, and observe whether the solid disappears
  4. Ask more classmates and go with the majority
Correct answer: C
A prediction is tested with a controlled experiment: a small measured amount in a measured volume of water, stirred and observed. Colour alone, or opinion, is not evidence of solubility.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: teacher demonstration with the extra NaCl sample before the structured experiment
Q2. Before the experiment, the teacher shows learners how to add only one level spatula tip (about 0.1 g) of salt to 5 mL of water. Why is this important?
  1. Adding more salt makes every salt become insoluble
  2. Only a tiny amount can ever dissolve in any water
  3. A larger amount of salt would make the test tube break
  4. Too much salt can go beyond the solubility limit, so a soluble salt may wrongly look insoluble
Correct answer: D
If too much salt is added, some solid is left over even for a soluble salt. This would give a wrong conclusion, so a small, equal amount is used for every tube.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: groups record whether the CaCO₃ mixture stays cloudy after stirring and after 2 minutes
Q3. A group stirs calcium carbonate in 5 mL of distilled water ten times and leaves it for 2 minutes. The mixture is still cloudy. What is the best conclusion?
  1. Calcium carbonate is soluble but needs to be stirred for an hour
  2. Calcium carbonate is insoluble; the cloudiness is solid still suspended in the water
  3. Calcium carbonate has dissolved and made a cloudy solution
  4. No conclusion is possible because the water was distilled
Correct answer: B
A soluble salt disappears to give a clear solution. A mixture that stays cloudy after stirring and standing shows that undissolved solid remains, so the salt is insoluble.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: pairs identify patterns in the class chart (carbonates highlighted) and draft solubility rules
Q4. A learner writes the rule: "All carbonates are insoluble in water." Which result from the solubility experiment shows this rule is wrong?
  1. Calcium carbonate stayed cloudy in water
  2. Sodium chloride dissolved in water
  3. Sodium carbonate dissolved in water
  4. Lead sulphate stayed as a solid in water
Correct answer: C
Sodium carbonate is a carbonate that dissolved. This shows carbonates are not all insoluble: most are insoluble, but sodium, potassium and ammonium carbonates are soluble.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: reading card of standard solubility rules, comparing with learners' drafted rules
Q5. Which statement about the solubility of sulphates in water is correct?
  1. All sulphates are soluble, including lead(II) sulphate
  2. All sulphates are insoluble
  3. Only coloured sulphates are soluble
  4. Most sulphates are soluble, but lead(II) sulphate is an insoluble exception
Correct answer: D
The solubility rules state that most sulphates are soluble, with exceptions such as lead(II) sulphate and barium sulphate. Copper sulphate dissolves, but lead sulphate does not.
Use after: DQB phase — Explain and DQB: two-sentence answer to the Lesson 1 question about table salt, githeri water and calcium carbonate
Q6. Which answer best explains, using evidence, why table salt dissolves in githeri water but calcium carbonate (limestone) does not?
  1. Calcium carbonate is a bigger lump than table salt
  2. Both are white solids, so they should behave the same way
  3. Githeri water only dissolves salts that are used in cooking
  4. Sodium chloride is a chloride, and most chlorides are soluble; in the experiment it dissolved. Calcium carbonate is a carbonate, and most carbonates are insoluble; in the experiment it stayed cloudy.
Correct answer: D
A good answer links the salt type to the solubility rule and to the experimental result for each salt: NaCl dissolved and CaCO₃ stayed cloudy.
Use after: Model Building phase — Model Building: adding the Solubility column and the dashed arrow 'Why does it clump if there is no liquid water?' on the NH₄NO₃ box
Q7. Ammonium nitrate (in CAN fertiliser) dissolves in liquid water. A bag of CAN was left open overnight in a dry storeroom and the granules became wet and sticky, though no liquid water was added. What does this tell us?
  1. Solubility in liquid water alone does not explain the clumping, so the question remains open
  2. Solubility in liquid water fully explains the clumping, so there is nothing more to investigate
  3. The fertiliser must have been rained on during the night
  4. Ammonium nitrate must be insoluble, because no liquid water was present
Correct answer: A
The experiment shows ammonium nitrate is soluble in liquid water, but no liquid water was present in the storeroom. So the overnight clumping is still unexplained and needs further investigation.
