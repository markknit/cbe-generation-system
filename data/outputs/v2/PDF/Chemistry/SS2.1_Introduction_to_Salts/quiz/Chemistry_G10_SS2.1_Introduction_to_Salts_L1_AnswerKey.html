Q1. A bag of CAN fertiliser was left open overnight in a school storeroom. Washing soda crystals were left on a laboratory bench, and table salt was kept in a kitchen store. Which description matches what learners observed the next morning?
  1. The fertiliser granules stayed dry, the washing soda became wet and sticky, and the table salt clumped together.
  2. The fertiliser granules turned into a fine powder, the washing soda stayed unchanged, and the table salt developed a white crust.
  3. All three substances clumped together and felt wet and sticky.
  4. The fertiliser granules clumped and felt wet, the washing soda crystals shrank and had a white powdery crust, and the table salt looked unchanged.
Correct answer: D
The observations were: fertiliser clumped and wet, washing soda shrunk with a white powdery crust, and table salt unchanged. The other options mix up which substance did what.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: structured observation table and the KICD definition card
Q2. Which statement gives the definition of a salt that learners annotated on their observation tables?
  1. A salt is only the white substance we add to food, sodium chloride.
  2. A salt is any white solid that dissolves in water.
  3. A salt is a substance formed when a hydrogen atom in an acid is fully or partially replaced by a positive ion.
  4. A salt is a substance formed when the hydroxide ion of a base is replaced by a hydrogen atom.
Correct answer: C
A salt forms when hydrogen in an acid is fully or partially replaced by a positive ion. The other options describe colour, solubility or table salt only, which do not define salts in general.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: annotating the observation table to identify which acid and positive ion formed each sample
Q3. Table salt, NaCl, is a salt. Which acid and which positive ion would form it, by replacing hydrogen in the acid?
  1. Hydrochloric acid, with the hydrogen replaced by Na⁺
  2. Nitric acid, with the hydrogen replaced by Na⁺
  3. Sulphuric acid, with the hydrogen replaced by Na⁺
  4. Hydrochloric acid, with the hydrogen replaced by Cl⁻
Correct answer: A
NaCl comes from hydrochloric acid, HCl, where H is replaced by the positive ion Na⁺. Cl⁻ is a negative ion and cannot replace hydrogen in the definition. Sulphuric and nitric acids would give sulphates and nitrates.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: 16-card sorting activity and the exit check on NaHCO₃
Q4. Sodium hydrogen carbonate, NaHCO₃, is used in baking. How is it classified?
  1. A normal salt, because it contains no OH⁻ ion
  2. A double salt, because its formula contains two different elements besides oxygen
  3. A basic salt, because it contains sodium, which comes from a base
  4. An acidic salt, because only part of the hydrogen in the acid has been replaced and one H remains
Correct answer: D
NaHCO₃ still contains one hydrogen from carbonic acid, so the hydrogen is only partially replaced. That makes it an acidic salt. Having no OH⁻ does not make a salt normal.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: 16-card sorting activity (double salt cards)
Q5. Potassium aluminium sulphate (alum), KAl(SO₄)₂·12H₂O, is used to clean water. Which type of salt is it, and why?
  1. A double salt, because it contains two different positive ions, K⁺ and Al³⁺
  2. A basic salt, because it contains water of crystallisation
  3. A normal salt, because it has only one formula
  4. An acidic salt, because it contains sulphate ions
Correct answer: A
Alum has two different metal (positive) ions, potassium and aluminium, so it is a double salt. Having one formula, containing water or containing sulphate does not decide the class.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: closing discussion returning to the phenomenon after the card sort ('classification alone does NOT explain the phenomenon')
Q6. Table salt (NaCl), washing soda (Na₂CO₃) and the salt in CAN fertiliser (NH₄NO₃) are all normal salts. Yet overnight in the open they behaved differently. What does this tell us?
  1. Table salt must not be a real salt, because it did not change.
  2. Being a normal salt means a salt will always stay unchanged in the open.
  3. Classifying the salts as normal does not by itself explain the different behaviour, so we still need to investigate other properties of salts.
  4. The fertiliser and the washing soda must be acidic salts, because they changed.
Correct answer: C
All three are normal salts but behaved differently, so classification alone does not explain the phenomenon. The class still needs to investigate other properties. This is the open question carried into the next lesson.
Use after: DQB phase — DQB: posting pink question sticky notes on the Driving Question Board
Q7. Learners are posting pink 'question' sticky notes on the Driving Question Board about the fertiliser, washing soda and table salt. Which note is a good phenomenon-linked question?
  1. I wonder what a salt is.
  2. I wonder why the storeroom door is always locked.
  3. I wonder why CAN fertiliser clumps and feels wet overnight but table salt does not.
  4. I wonder how much a bag of fertiliser costs.
Correct answer: C
A strong DQB question refers directly to the puzzle of the three salts behaving differently. 'What is a salt' is too vague, and the cost of fertiliser or the storeroom door is unrelated to the phenomenon.
