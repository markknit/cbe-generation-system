Q1. Granules of CAN fertiliser were left in an open bag in a school storeroom overnight. By morning they had clumped together and felt wet and sticky, although no water was added. Which property of CAN explains why Rift Valley farmers must store it in sealed bags?
  1. It loses its water of crystallisation to the air (it is efflorescent)
  2. It evaporates slowly when left in the open
  3. It reacts with oxygen in the air to form a sticky oil
  4. It absorbs so much water vapour from the air that it becomes wet (it is deliquescent)
Correct answer: D
CAN takes in water vapour from humid air and becomes wet and clumped, so it must be kept sealed. Loss of water of crystallisation (efflorescence) would produce a dry powder, not a sticky mass.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: Expert Group 1 (Agriculture), effects of too much inorganic fertiliser and ways to reduce them
Q2. Heavy rain washes extra inorganic fertiliser (nitrates and phosphates) from maize farms into rivers that flow into Lake Victoria. What is a likely effect on the lake?
  1. Fish numbers increase because the water has fewer plants
  2. The lake water becomes more acidic because sodium chloride is added
  3. Algal blooms and water hyacinth grow rapidly
  4. The water becomes harder because calcium carbonate dissolves
Correct answer: C
Nitrates and phosphates are plant nutrients. When they run into the lake they feed algal blooms and water hyacinth, which harm the lake and its fish.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: Expert Group 1 (Agriculture), ways to reduce the effects of too much fertiliser
Q3. Which action would best reduce the harm that excess inorganic fertiliser does to rivers and soil?
  1. Stop using manure and compost and use only inorganic fertiliser
  2. Spread the fertiliser on bare, sloping ground so the rain can wash it in
  3. Apply only the recommended amount at the right time, and also use manure and compost
  4. Apply double the amount before the rains so the crop always has enough
Correct answer: C
Using only the recommended amount at the right time, and adding manure and compost, means less fertiliser is left to be washed into rivers. Planting along the contours also helps.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: Expert Group 2 (Food Industry), sodium chloride for preserving omena and tilapia
Q4. Fishmongers at Dunga Beach on Lake Victoria rub sodium chloride onto fresh tilapia to preserve it. Which statement best explains why this works?
  1. Every salt is poisonous to bacteria, so any salt would preserve the fish equally well
  2. The salt reacts with the fish to make carbon dioxide, which kills bacteria
  3. The salt draws water out of the fish and out of microbes, so bacteria cannot grow well
  4. The salt is deliquescent, so it makes the fish wetter and keeps it fresh
Correct answer: C
Sodium chloride dissolves in the water on the fish and draws water out (an osmotic effect). Without water, bacteria cannot grow. Not all salts preserve food, so the claim that any salt works is a misconception.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: Expert Group 3 (Medicine), oral rehydration salts and why ionic composition matters
Q5. A child with diarrhoea loses water and dissolved ions from the body. A nurse at a rural dispensary mixes oral rehydration salts (ORS) in water for the child. Which salts in ORS help to replace the lost ions?
  1. Barium sulphate and titanium dioxide
  2. Ammonium sulphate and single super phosphate
  3. Sodium carbonate and calcium carbonate
  4. Sodium chloride and potassium chloride (with sodium citrate)
Correct answer: D
ORS contains sodium chloride, potassium chloride and sodium citrate, with glucose, to replace the sodium and potassium ions and water lost in diarrhoea. The other choices are pigments, glass or laundry salts, and fertilisers.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: Expert Group 4 (Paper and Paint Industries), barium sulphate as a white pigment and the insolubility rule
Q6. Barium sulphate is used as a white pigment in paint. Which property makes it suitable for this use?
  1. It is very soluble in water, so it spreads evenly through the wall
  2. It is efflorescent, so it forms a powdery layer on the wall
  3. It is deliquescent, so it keeps the paint wet
  4. It is insoluble in water, so it stays as a solid and does not dissolve or wash away
Correct answer: D
Barium sulphate is insoluble in water, according to the solubility rules. It therefore stays as a white solid pigment in the paint and does not dissolve or wash off.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: Expert Group 5 (Glass Industry), sodium carbonate (soda ash) as a flux
Q7. Soda ash (sodium carbonate) from Lake Magadi is added to sand (silica) when glass is made. What is its main job?
  1. It raises the melting point so the glass is stronger
  2. It softens the glass so that it never breaks
  3. It acts as a flux that lowers the melting point of the silica
  4. It makes the glass white by acting as a pigment
Correct answer: C
Silica melts at a very high temperature. Sodium carbonate is a flux: it lowers the melting point, which saves energy and makes glass-making easier.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: Expert Group 6 (Laundry Industry), washing soda as a water softener and the precipitation link to Lesson 5
Q8. Washing soda (sodium carbonate) softens hard water in laundry. Which statement explains how?
  1. Carbonate ions react with Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions in the water to form insoluble CaCO₃ and MgCO₃, which are removed as solids
  2. Washing soda adds more calcium and magnesium ions, so the soap lathers better
  3. Sodium ions in washing soda dissolve the calcium and magnesium ions in the water
  4. The washing soda crystals lose water and absorb the calcium and magnesium ions as gases
Correct answer: A
Carbonate ions combine with Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ to form insoluble carbonates, which precipitate out. This is the same kind of precipitation reaction studied earlier. Once these ions are removed, the water is soft.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: Whole-Class Synthesis using the Property → Use → Phenomenon framework (washing soda)
Q9. A container of washing soda (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O) left open on a laboratory bench developed a white powdery crust and the crystals looked smaller. Which statement correctly explains this, and what does it mean for storage?
  1. It is efflorescent: it loses water of crystallisation to the air, so it should be kept in a closed container
  2. It is hygroscopic and gains mass, so it should be kept in the sun
  3. It is deliquescent: it absorbs water from the air and dissolves, so it should be kept open to dry
  4. It is insoluble, so it reacts with oxygen to form the crust
Correct answer: A
Washing soda is a hydrated salt that loses some of its water of crystallisation to the air. This leaves a white powdery crust and makes the crystals shrink. A closed container keeps the crystals from changing. This is a separate property from deliquescence in CAN.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: Whole-Class Synthesis using the Property → Use → Phenomenon framework (table salt)
Q10. Table salt (NaCl) in a kitchen store was left open in ordinary air and looked completely unchanged, unlike CAN fertiliser and washing soda. Which statement best explains this?
  1. It is insoluble in water, so it never changes
  2. It neither absorbs enough water from ordinary air to become wet nor loses water of crystallisation, so it can be stored open
  3. It is deliquescent, so it always becomes a clear solution overnight
  4. It is not a salt, so air has no effect on it
Correct answer: B
Table salt is neither deliquescent nor efflorescent in ordinary air, and it has no water of crystallisation to lose. It therefore stays unchanged, unlike CAN, which absorbs water, and washing soda, which loses water.
