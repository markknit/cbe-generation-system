Q1. Salt (sodium chloride) is sprinkled into a cooking fire and the flame glows yellow. Which explanation is best?
  1. The sodium nucleus breaks apart in the heat and gives out yellow light.
  2. Sodium atoms melt in the fire and the liquid sodium itself glows yellow.
  3. Sodium electrons leave the atom for good and the yellow colour is the smoke from the escaped electrons.
  4. Sodium electrons absorb energy from the flame and jump to a higher energy level. When they fall back, they release that energy as yellow light.
Correct answer: D
Flame colour comes from electrons. They absorb heat, move to higher energy levels (excited state), then fall back and release the energy as light of a particular colour.

Q2. A sodium atom (Z = 11) has the electron configuration 1s²2s²2p⁶3s¹. Which orbital holds the outermost (valence) electron that gets excited in a flame?
  1. 3p
  2. 3s
  3. 1s
  4. 2p
Correct answer: B
The configuration ends in 3s¹. The single electron in the 3s orbital is the outermost (valence) electron, and it is the one excited in the flame.
Use after: Observe phase — Evidence Station A: Dalton, Rutherford and Bohr model diagrams with key evidence
Q3. Which atomic model was the first to place electrons in fixed energy levels (shells) around a small central nucleus?
  1. Dalton's solid sphere model
  2. Rutherford's nuclear model
  3. Bohr's shell model
  4. Dalton's model after adding a nucleus
Correct answer: C
Rutherford showed there is a small, dense nucleus. Bohr then arranged the electrons in fixed energy levels (shells). Dalton's model had no electrons at all.

Q4. Sodium gives a yellow flame and potassium gives a violet flame. What is the best reason for the difference?
  1. Potassium atoms have more neutrons, so their flame burns hotter and looks violet.
  2. The two flames use different fuels, so the colour is caused by the firewood.
  3. Potassium electrons do not move in a flame, so only sodium makes light.
  4. The electrons in sodium and potassium have different energy levels, so they release different amounts of energy as light, and different energies give different colours.
Correct answer: D
Each element has its own electron arrangement and energy levels. The energy released when an excited electron falls back differs between sodium and potassium, so the colour of the light differs.
Use after: Explain phase — Atomic Model Journey poster: isotope panel with the RAM calculation for chlorine
Q5. Chlorine has two isotopes: Cl-35 (75% of atoms) and Cl-37 (25% of atoms). What is the relative atomic mass (RAM) of chlorine?
  1. 35.5
  2. 35.0
  3. 36.0
  4. 36.5
Correct answer: A
RAM is a weighted average: (75 × 35 + 25 × 37) ÷ 100 = (2625 + 925) ÷ 100 = 35.5. Taking a simple average (36.0) ignores that Cl-35 is more common.
Use after: DQB phase — Driving Question Board closure: voting on the wonder question 'Are all atoms of the same element identical?'
Q6. A learner on the Driving Question Board asked: 'Are all atoms of the same element identical?' Which answer is correct?
  1. No, because atoms of the same element can have different numbers of neutrons. These are called isotopes.
  2. Yes, because neutrons do not affect the atom in any way.
  3. No, because the number of protons can change between atoms of the same element.
  4. Yes, because atoms of one element always have the same mass number.
Correct answer: A
Atoms of one element have the same number of protons, but can have different numbers of neutrons. This gives different mass numbers, for example Cl-35 and Cl-37.
Use after: Model Building phase — Exit slip Task 1: electron configuration of potassium (K, Z = 19)
Q7. Potassium has atomic number 19. Which is its correct electron configuration?
  1. 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶4s²
  2. 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁵4s²
  3. 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶4s¹
  4. 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶4p¹
Correct answer: C
The orbitals fill in the order 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s: 2+2+6+2+6+1 = 19 electrons. The last electron goes into 4s, not 4p. The option ending 4s² has 20 electrons.
Use after: End of lesson — Whole-lesson close: class answer to the driving question about flame colours and electron arrangement
Q8. Why can the colour of a flame be used to help identify an element such as sodium, potassium or copper?
  1. The flame colour depends only on how hot the Bunsen burner is.
  2. The colour is decided by the number of neutrons, which is the same in every atom of an element.
  3. Each element has its own electron energy levels, so it always gives off light of the same colours. The colour acts like a fingerprint.
  4. All elements give the same colour, but the brightness shows which element it is.
Correct answer: C
The energy levels of electrons are fixed for each element. The light released is therefore repeatable and specific to the element, which is why flame colour is a fingerprint of electron arrangement.
