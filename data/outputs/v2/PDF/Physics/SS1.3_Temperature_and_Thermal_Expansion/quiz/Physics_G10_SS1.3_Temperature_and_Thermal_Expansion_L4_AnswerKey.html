Q1. A 500 ml metal sufuria is filled to the brim with water at 20 °C and heated on a jiko to 80 °C. What happens to the volume of the water?
  1. It increases slightly, because water expands when heated in this temperature range
  2. It stays the same, because the sufuria holds the water in
  3. It decreases, because the water particles pack closer together when heated
  4. It first decreases and then increases, because water is always densest at 20 °C
Correct answer: A
Water heated from 20 °C to 80 °C expands like most liquids. The unusual behaviour of water only occurs below 4 °C.
Use after: Observe phase — Part A: Derivation Card, extending linear expansion to area and volume
Q2. A square plate has side L₀. After heating, each side becomes L₀(1 + αΔT), so the new area is A = L₀²(1 + αΔT)². Why can we write A ≈ L₀²(1 + 2αΔT) and so say β ≈ 2α?
  1. Because the plate expands in only one direction when heated
  2. Because the area of a square is always twice its side length
  3. Because α is a very small number, so the (αΔT)² term is tiny and can be ignored
  4. Because the (αΔT)² term is negative and cancels the 2αΔT term
Correct answer: C
Expanding gives 1 + 2αΔT + (αΔT)². Since α is about 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹, (αΔT)² is extremely small compared with 2αΔT and can be dropped, so β ≈ 2α.
Use after: Observe phase — Part A: bridge-deck slab problem (i), 12 m × 4 m slab heated from 15 °C to 45 °C
Q3. A rectangular concrete bridge-deck slab is 12 m × 4 m at 15 °C. The linear expansivity of the concrete is α = 10 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹. By how much does its area increase when it reaches 45 °C in the midday sun?
  1. 0.0144 m²
  2. 0.0432 m²
  3. 0.288 m²
  4. 0.0288 m²
Correct answer: D
β = 2α = 20 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹, A₀ = 12 × 4 = 48 m², ΔT = 30 K. ΔA = βA₀ΔT = 20 × 10⁻⁶ × 48 × 30 ≈ 0.029 m².
Use after: Observe phase — Part A: homework problem (ii), aluminium sufuria heated from 20 °C to 100 °C
Q4. A 2-litre (2000 ml) aluminium sufuria has linear expansivity α = 23 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹. It is heated from 20 °C to 100 °C. By how much does its internal volume increase?
  1. 110.4 ml
  2. 11.04 ml
  3. 3.68 ml
  4. 7.36 ml
Correct answer: B
γ = 3α = 69 × 10⁻⁶ K⁻¹. ΔV = γV₀ΔT = 69 × 10⁻⁶ × 2000 × 80 ≈ 11 ml. Using α or 2α instead of 3α gives the smaller wrong values.
Use after: Observe phase — Part B: Flask-and-Capillary Experiment, plotting Temperature vs. Capillary Height
Q5. A group cools a flask of water fitted with a narrow capillary tube. The water column height in the capillary is: 10 °C: 52 mm; 8 °C: 48 mm; 6 °C: 45 mm; 4 °C: 44 mm; 2 °C: 45 mm; 0 °C: 46 mm. At which temperature is the water densest?
  1. 10 °C, because the column is highest there
  2. 2 °C, because the column starts rising there
  3. 4 °C, because the column is lowest, so the volume is smallest
  4. 0 °C, because it is the coldest reading
Correct answer: C
The capillary column height shows volume. The lowest column (44 mm) means the smallest volume for the same mass, so the greatest density, at 4 °C. Below 4 °C the column rises again.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: Claim–Evidence–Reasoning (CER) frame, 'Why did the sealed bottle crack?'
Q6. A sealed glass bottle full of water cracks in a freezer. Which statement best explains the main cause of the crack?
  1. The water expands by about 9% when it freezes at 0 °C, and the sealed glass cannot stretch
  2. The tiny expansion between 4 °C and 0 °C (about 0.01%) alone produces enough force to crack the glass
  3. The glass contracts much more than the water when it gets cold
  4. The water shrinks as it freezes and pulls the glass walls inwards
Correct answer: A
The main cause is the roughly 9% increase in volume when water changes state to ice at 0 °C. The 4 °C to 0 °C expansion is very small and is only the first sign of the unusual behaviour.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: scaffolded question 2, volume increase when 500 ml of water freezes at 0 °C
Q7. Freezing increases the volume of water by about 9%. By how much does the volume increase when 500 ml of water freezes at 0 °C?
  1. 45 ml
  2. 545 ml
  3. 4.5 ml
  4. 9 ml
Correct answer: A
Increase = 9% of 500 ml = 0.09 × 500 = 45 ml, so the ice occupies about 545 ml. The formula ΔV = γV₀ΔT cannot be used because freezing is a change of state.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: scaffolded question 3, molecular diagram of ice hexagonal lattice
Q8. Why does ice take up more space than the same mass of liquid water?
  1. In ice the molecules sit in an open hexagonal lattice with larger gaps between them
  2. Extra water molecules are added to the ice as it forms
  3. The water molecules become larger when they freeze
  4. In ice the molecules move faster and push each other apart
Correct answer: A
The number and size of the molecules do not change. In the hexagonal ice lattice the molecules are held in an open arrangement with greater spacing, so the same mass takes up more volume.
Use after: DQB phase — Driving Question Board: Zone B 'What EXPLAINS the crack' causal chain arrow card
Q9. Which causal chain correctly explains why a sealed water bottle cracks in a freezer?
  1. Water freezes at 4 °C, becomes less dense than glass, and the metal cap pushes the glass until it cracks
  2. Water cools to 4 °C and expands by 9%, then freezes and shrinks, the glass is pulled inwards and cracks
  3. Water cools and keeps shrinking until it freezes, the glass shrinks more, the glass is squeezed and cracks
  4. Water cools to 4 °C (smallest volume), then freezes at 0 °C and expands by about 9%, pressure on the glass rises above its tensile strength, and the glass cracks
Correct answer: D
Water reaches minimum volume at 4 °C, then expands, and expands by about 9% when it freezes at 0 °C. A sealed bottle cannot stretch, so the pressure rises above the tensile strength of the glass and it cracks.
