Q1. A steel spring stretches by 4 cm when a 1 N load hangs from it. The spring obeys Hooke's Law. What extension do you expect when a 4 N load hangs from the same spring?
  1. 8 cm
  2. 4 cm
  3. 16 cm
  4. 5 cm
Correct answer: C
Extension is directly proportional to force. 4 N is 4 times 1 N, so the extension is 4 × 4 cm = 16 cm. An answer of 8 cm comes from doubling instead of quadrupling.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: spring and slotted masses investigation, results table (Force (N) | Extension (cm))
Q2. A group measures the natural length of a spring as 20.0 cm. After adding a 2 N load, the spring's new length is 26.5 cm. What is the extension of the spring?
  1. 13.25 cm
  2. 6.5 cm
  3. 26.5 cm
  4. 46.5 cm
Correct answer: B
Extension = new length − natural length = 26.5 − 20.0 = 6.5 cm. Recording the total length (26.5 cm) is the common error the teacher checks for in the results table.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: teacher demonstration of the setup at the front bench (reading the ruler)
Q3. When reading the length of a spring against a metre rule, why should your eye be level with the bottom of the spring coil?
  1. To make the load heavier
  2. To avoid parallax error in the reading
  3. To make the spring stop stretching
  4. To increase the spring constant
Correct answer: B
Reading from above or below the mark gives a wrong value because of parallax error. Keeping the eye level with the coil gives an accurate length.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: calculating k from the force-extension graph and the F/x ratio check
Q4. In a Hooke's Law investigation, a steel spring is stretched by 0.12 m when a 3 N force is applied. It is still within its elastic limit. What is the spring constant k?
  1. 25 N/m
  2. 4 N/m
  3. 0.25 N/m
  4. 36 N/m
Correct answer: A
k = F ÷ x = 3 N ÷ 0.12 m = 25 N/m. Using 12 cm without converting to metres gives 0.25 N/m, which is wrong.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: worked example using F = kx (spring with k = 20 N/m)
Q5. A spring has a spring constant k = 20 N/m. A force of 5 N is applied to it, within its elastic limit. What is the extension of the spring?
  1. 100 m
  2. 4 m
  3. 0.25 m
  4. 25 m
Correct answer: C
Using F = kx, x = F ÷ k = 5 N ÷ 20 N/m = 0.25 m. Multiplying F by k gives 100, and inverting the ratio gives 4; both are wrong.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: introducing the elastic limit and annotating graphs (revisiting the prediction slips)
Q6. A spring is loaded with more and more mass until the force-extension graph starts to curve away from a straight line. What does this point on the graph tell you about the spring?
  1. The spring has become full and will not stretch any more
  2. The spring constant k has doubled
  3. The spring has returned to its natural length
  4. The spring has reached its elastic limit, and beyond it the spring will not return to its natural length
Correct answer: D
The point where the graph curves away from linearity marks the elastic limit. Beyond it the spring is permanently deformed and stays longer than its natural length. The spring keeps stretching; it does not stop.
Use after: DQB phase — Driving Question Board: PARTIAL ANSWER linking k and elastic limit to the Kericho rope that snapped
Q7. Two ropes of similar appearance carry the same heavy load. One rope returns to its original length. The other stretches and then snaps. Using what you learnt about springs, which statement is the best explanation for the rope that snapped?
  1. It probably had a lower spring constant k and a lower elastic limit, so the load took it past its elastic limit into permanent deformation
  2. It had a higher elastic limit than the other rope, so it deformed permanently
  3. The ropes look alike, so they must have the same k and the same elastic limit
  4. Elastic limit has nothing to do with whether a rope snaps
Correct answer: A
A material with a lower k and a lower elastic limit is more likely to be taken past its elastic limit by the same load. This leads to permanent deformation and failure. Looks alone do not tell us a material's k or elastic limit.
Use after: Model Building phase — Model Building: Rope Model revision, dashed line at F = 80 kg × 10 m/s² on the force-extension graphs
Q8. A sack of tea leaves has a mass of 80 kg. Taking g = 10 N/kg, what is the downward force (weight) the sack exerts on a bridge rope?
  1. 8000 N
  2. 800 N
  3. 8 N
  4. 80 N
Correct answer: B
Weight = mass × g = 80 kg × 10 N/kg = 800 N. This is the force drawn as the vertical dashed line on the rope force-extension graphs.
