Q1. A kettle is rated 3000 W and is used on the 240 V mains. What current does it take? (Use I = P/V.)
  1. 12.5 A
  2. 0.08 A
  3. 720 000 A
  4. 3240 A
Correct answer: A
I = P/V = 3000 ÷ 240 = 12.5 A. Dividing the wrong way round gives 0.08 A, and multiplying or adding the numbers is not what P = IV says.
Use after: Observe phase — Part A (calculation from data): adding the currents and comparing with the 13 A fuse
Q2. A 3000 W kettle (12.5 A) and a 2000 W heater (8.33 A) are plugged into one extension socket on the 240 V mains and switched on together. What is the total current drawn from the socket?
  1. 20.8 A
  2. 10.4 A
  3. 4.2 A
  4. 12.5 A
Correct answer: A
The appliances are in parallel, so their currents add: 12.5 A + 8.33 A = 20.8 A. Subtracting, averaging or using only the larger current are common errors.
Use after: Observe phase — Part A (calculation from data): teacher question 'Why do we ADD the currents and not the resistances here?'
Q3. Two appliances are plugged into the same 240 V socket and work in parallel. Why do we ADD their currents to find the total current from the socket?
  1. Each appliance has its own branch and takes its own current from the same 240 V, so the supply must provide both currents together.
  2. The fuse stores current from the first appliance and passes it to the second.
  3. The voltage of the socket rises when a second appliance is plugged in, so the current rises too.
  4. The resistances of the two appliances add up, which makes the current bigger.
Correct answer: A
In a parallel circuit each branch is connected across the same voltage and carries its own current. The supply current is the sum of the branch currents. Voltage does not rise when appliances are added.
Use after: Explain phase — CER explanation: Claim that the 13 A fuse blows when the kettle and heater share the socket
Q4. A 13 A fuse is in a plug. A kettle and a heater together draw 20.8 A from this plug. Which statement best explains what happens?
  1. The fuse has 13 V across it, so it blows when the voltage is too low.
  2. The fuse wire melts, because the current is more than 13 A and the thin wire heats up and breaks the circuit.
  3. The fuse uses up the extra current so that less electricity reaches the appliances.
  4. Nothing happens, because the fuse only stops current in series circuits.
Correct answer: B
20.8 A is above the 13 A rating. The current heats the thin fuse wire, which is the weakest part, so it melts and opens the circuit before the cable overheats. A fuse rating is a current, not a voltage, and a fuse does not use up or reduce current.
Use after: Observe phase — Part C: inspection station 1, the dismantled 3-pin plug with fuse and live, neutral and earth wires
Q5. In a 3-pin plug, which wire must the fuse be connected to, and why?
  1. The neutral wire, because the neutral carries the most current.
  2. Any wire, because a fuse works the same wherever it is placed.
  3. The live wire, so that when the fuse melts the appliance is cut off from the 240 V supply and is not left live.
  4. The earth wire, because the fuse must be joined to the ground.
Correct answer: C
The fuse and switch go on the live (brown) wire. When they break the circuit, the appliance is disconnected from the 240 V live supply and is not left live.
Use after: Explain phase — Application question (c): earthing and the faulty live wire touching the kettle body
Q6. A faulty live wire touches the metal body of an electric kettle that is connected to an earth wire. How does the earth wire protect a person using the kettle?
  1. It stores the extra voltage in the ground until the kettle is switched off.
  2. It lets the current flow through the person to the ground so the kettle keeps working.
  3. It gives the fault current a low-resistance path to the ground, so a large current flows and blows the fuse, and the metal body does not stay live.
  4. It increases the resistance of the kettle so that less current flows.
Correct answer: C
The earth wire gives a low-resistance path to the ground. A large fault current then flows and blows the fuse, cutting off the live supply so the metal body cannot give a shock.
Use after: Explain phase — Application question (a): Why is a 13 A fuse better than a thick nail in the plug?
Q7. Why is a proper 13 A fuse a safer choice in a plug than a thick nail?
  1. A nail would stop the current from flowing at all.
  2. A nail has a lower resistance, so it carries more current and is better for appliances.
  3. A fuse wire is thin, so it heats and melts when the current is too high, but a thick nail would not melt and the cable could overheat and start a fire.
  4. A nail would reduce the voltage of the mains, so appliances would not work.
Correct answer: C
A fuse is designed to be the weakest, thinnest part, so it melts first on overload. A thick nail would not melt, so the cable could get very hot (as with thin cables in Lesson 6) and cause a fire.
Use after: End of lesson — Explain phase: quick exit slip on the 2000 W heater and 1000 W iron sharing a 13 A socket
Q8. A 2000 W heater and a 1000 W iron share one socket on the 240 V mains, and the plug has a 13 A fuse. What is the total current, and will the fuse blow?
  1. 12.5 A; the fuse will blow because the two appliances are in parallel.
  2. 0.08 A; the fuse will not blow.
  3. 12.5 A; the fuse will not blow, because the current is just below 13 A.
  4. 20.8 A; the fuse will blow.
Correct answer: C
Total power = 2000 + 1000 = 3000 W, so I = 3000 ÷ 240 = 12.5 A (or 8.33 A + 4.17 A). This is just below 13 A, so the fuse should not blow, but it is close.
