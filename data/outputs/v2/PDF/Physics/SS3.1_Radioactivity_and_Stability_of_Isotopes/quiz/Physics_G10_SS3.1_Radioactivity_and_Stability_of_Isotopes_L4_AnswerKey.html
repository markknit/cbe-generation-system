Q1. In the dice simulation, 100 dice are rolled and every die showing 6 is removed. In Round 1, 17 dice are removed. In Round 3, only 12 are removed. Why does the number removed fall from round to round?
  1. The dice slowly become less likely to land on 6 as they are rolled more times
  2. The probability of a 6 drops by a fixed amount each round
  3. The dice that are left have used up some of their chance of decaying
  4. Fewer dice are left to roll, and each remaining die still has the same 1-in-6 chance of being removed
Correct answer: D
The chance for each die stays 1 in 6, but there are fewer dice to roll, so fewer 6s appear. In the same way, fewer nuclei decay per unit time as the sample shrinks, even though each nucleus has a constant chance of decaying.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: plotting the decay curve and reading off the half-life (dashed line at 50 dice remaining)
Q2. A radioactive sample has 80 undecayed nuclei at time 0. A decay curve for it shows 40 nuclei left at 6 days and 20 nuclei left at 12 days. What is the half-life of the sample?
  1. 6 days
  2. 3 days
  3. 12 days
  4. 40 days
Correct answer: A
Half-life is the time for the number of undecayed nuclei to fall to half. The number falls from 80 to 40 in 6 days, so the half-life is 6 days. It halves again from 40 to 20 in the next 6 days.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: formative question 'If you started with 200 dice, would your half-life change?'
Q3. One group starts the dice simulation with 100 dice. Another group starts with 200 dice. Each die still has a 1-in-6 chance of being removed on every roll. Compared with the group with 100 dice, what will the group with 200 dice find for its half-life in rolls?
  1. About the same number of rolls, because half-life does not depend on the starting number
  2. Exactly the same number of dice removed in every round
  3. About twice as long, because there are twice as many dice to remove
  4. About half as long, because more dice means more 6s per roll
Correct answer: A
Half-life depends only on the chance of decay for each nucleus (or die), not on the size of the sample. Both groups should find about 3.8 rolls, even though the 200-dice group removes more dice per round.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: comparing curves and half-life values across groups ('What does the scatter tell us about individual nuclei?')
Q4. Different groups did the same dice simulation and got slightly different decay curves, but all of them found a half-life of about 3 to 5 rolls. What does this tell us about radioactive decay?
  1. Decay is not random, because every group got the same curve
  2. Decay of a single nucleus cannot be predicted, but the behaviour of a large number of nuclei follows a predictable pattern
  3. Each nucleus chooses when to decay, so every sample behaves differently
  4. The results are only reliable if the dice are rolled in the same order
Correct answer: A
Decay is random and spontaneous for each nucleus, so individual results scatter between groups. A large number of nuclei shows a consistent statistical pattern, which is why half-life is a reliable value.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: Problem 1 (Carbon dating at Lukenya Hill)
Q5. Archaeologists at Lukenya Hill in Machakos County find a charcoal fragment. It contains only one-eighth of the Carbon-14 activity of a living tree. The half-life of Carbon-14 is 5730 years. About how old is the charcoal?
  1. 11 460 years
  2. 716 years
  3. 17 190 years
  4. 45 840 years
Correct answer: C
One-eighth = (1/2)^3, so n = 3 half-lives have passed. Age = 3 x 5730 = 17 190 years.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: Problem 2 (Iodine-131 at KNH)
Q6. A patient at Kenyatta National Hospital is given Iodine-131, which has a half-life of 8 days. The dose starts with 8000 undecayed nuclei (simplified unit). How many undecayed nuclei remain after 32 days?
  1. 2000
  2. 0
  3. 1000
  4. 500
Correct answer: D
n = 32 / 8 = 4 half-lives. N = 8000 x (1/2)^4 = 8000 / 16 = 500. Dividing 8000 by 4 (giving 2000) is the common error of dividing by n instead of halving n times.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: journal paragraph connecting half-life to the pitchblende rock ('Uranium-238 has a half-life of approximately 4.5 billion years, which means...')
Q7. Uranium-238 in the pitchblende rock has a half-life of about 4.5 billion years. Which statement best explains why the rock still gives off radiation today?
  1. Uranium-238 nuclei decay very slowly, so an enormous number of undecayed nuclei are still left to decay
  2. The rock will stop emitting radiation very soon, because a half-life only lasts a short time
  3. Uranium-238 never decays, so the rock can never run out of radiation
  4. The rock keeps making new uranium-238 atoms to replace those that decay
Correct answer: A
A very long half-life means a very low decay rate for the whole sample. Even after billions of years, about half of the original nuclei are still undecayed, so the rock stays radioactive on a human timescale. The uranium does decay; it is not permanent.
Use after: Model Building phase — Model: adding the decay curve layer to the cumulative nuclear stability model
Q8. A group sketches a decay curve of the number of undecayed nuclei (vertical axis) against time (horizontal axis) for a radioactive sample. Which description of the curve is correct?
  1. A horizontal line, because the number of nuclei stays the same
  2. A curve that falls steeply at first and then flattens out, halving in each equal half-life interval
  3. A curve that rises steadily as the nuclei decay
  4. A straight line sloping down to zero at a steady rate
Correct answer: B
The number of nuclei halves in each half-life. The fall is therefore large at the start and gets smaller as fewer nuclei are left, giving an exponential decrease rather than a straight line.
