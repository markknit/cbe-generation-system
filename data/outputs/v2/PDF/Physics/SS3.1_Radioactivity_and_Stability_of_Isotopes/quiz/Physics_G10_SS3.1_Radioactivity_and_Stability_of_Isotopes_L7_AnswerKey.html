Use after: Predict phase — Predict: final complete explanation of the pitchblende rock and film phenomenon
Q1. A piece of pitchblende (uranium ore) is left on photographic film inside a light-proof, dark container. When the film is developed it is fogged. Which explanation is the most complete and correct?
  1. Uranium nuclei in the ore are unstable because their neutron-to-proton ratio is outside the belt of stability, so they decay and emit penetrating radiation that passes through the container and exposes the film.
  2. Chemical reactions between the rock and the air inside the container produce heat that darkens the film.
  3. The rock is stable, but it absorbs energy from the container and re-emits it as sound waves that affect the film.
  4. The rock stored sunlight during the day and slowly released it as light in the dark.
Correct answer: A
The unstable nuclei spontaneously decay and emit radiation (alpha, beta, gamma), and the more penetrating radiation passes through the container to expose the film. No light, heat or chemical reaction is involved.
Use after: Observe phase — Station 3: Industrial Thickness Gauging
Q2. A factory makes aluminium sheets and uses a radioactive source on one side of the moving sheet and a detector on the other side to check the thickness continuously. Why is a beta source the best choice?
  1. Beta particles are partly absorbed by the sheet, so the count reaching the detector goes up or down as the thickness changes.
  2. Alpha particles pass easily through metal sheets, so they give the strongest and clearest reading.
  3. Gamma rays are completely stopped by the sheet, so the detector reading shows the thickness directly.
  4. Beta particles are completely stopped by any sheet, so the detector reads zero when the thickness is correct.
Correct answer: A
Alpha is stopped by even thin material, so no reading would reach the detector, and gamma passes through almost unchanged, so the reading would hardly change. Beta is partly absorbed, so the detector count changes with thickness.
Use after: Observe phase — Station 4: Carbon-14 Dating (Koobi Fora fossil)
Q3. The Homo habilis fossil found at Koobi Fora, Lake Turkana, is about 1.8 million years old. The half-life of carbon-14 is 5,730 years. Why can carbon-14 dating NOT be used on this fossil?
  1. Carbon-14 has such a long half-life that it has barely started to decay in the fossil.
  2. Fossils gain new carbon-14 from the soil as they get older, so they always appear young.
  3. The fossil is more than 300 half-lives old, so almost no carbon-14 remains to be measured.
  4. Carbon-14 is only found in volcanic ash and not in living things.
Correct answer: C
1,800,000 ÷ 5,730 is about 314 half-lives, so the carbon-14 left is too small to measure. A isotope with a much longer half-life, such as potassium-40 (1.25 billion years), is more suitable.
Use after: Observe phase — Station 2: KALRO Crop Irradiation
Q4. At a research centre in Kenya, stored maize is treated with gamma rays from cobalt-60 to kill weevils. After the treatment, what is true about the maize?
  1. The maize is not made radioactive, because gamma rays pass through it and leave no radioactivity behind.
  2. The maize stays radioactive for as long as the half-life of cobalt-60, so it is unsafe to eat.
  3. Only the weevils become radioactive, while the maize itself is unaffected.
  4. The maize is radioactive for a few days and then becomes safe.
Correct answer: A
Gamma irradiation damages the cells of pests but does not make the food radioactive. This is different from food contaminated with radioactive dust.
Use after: Explain phase — Part A: Radiation Safety Design (inverse square law mini-lesson)
Q5. A nurse works near a gamma source that gives a dose rate of 80 µSv/h at a distance of 1 m. By the inverse square law, what is the dose rate if she moves to a distance of 2 m from the source?
  1. 40 µSv/h
  2. 10 µSv/h
  3. 20 µSv/h
  4. 160 µSv/h
Correct answer: C
Doubling the distance cuts the dose rate to one quarter: 80 ÷ 2² = 80 ÷ 4 = 20 µSv/h. Halving it to 40 µSv/h is the common mistake of treating it as a simple inverse relationship.
Use after: Explain phase — Part A: Radiation Safety Design (I-131 waste disposal half-life calculation)
Q6. Iodine-131 has a half-life of 8 days. Waste from a thyroid treatment unit is kept until its activity falls below 1% of the original. What is the shortest time, in whole half-lives, after which this is true?
  1. 8 days
  2. 80 days
  3. 48 days
  4. 56 days
Correct answer: D
After 6 half-lives the activity is 1/64 (about 1.6%), still above 1%. After 7 half-lives it is 1/128 (about 0.8%), which is below 1%. So 7 × 8 = 56 days.
Use after: Explain phase — Part A: Radiation Safety Design (ALARA safety plan for the nuclear medicine unit)
Q7. A radiographer in a Kenyan hospital works with a gamma source. Which set of actions best follows the ALARA principle (As Low As Reasonably Achievable)?
  1. Stand close to the source but only for a short time, because gamma rays are harmless without contact.
  2. Wear thin cotton clothing, since radiation is only dangerous if the source is touched.
  3. Spend as little time as possible near the source, stay as far away as practical, and use shielding such as lead or thick concrete.
  4. Use the strongest source possible and stay close so that the work is finished quickly, without shielding.
Correct answer: C
ALARA keeps exposure as low as reasonably achievable by combining less time, more distance and shielding. Gamma rays are very penetrating, so dense materials like lead or concrete are needed.
Use after: DQB phase — DQB Closure Ceremony: class consensus answer to the driving question (half-life element)
Q8. During the class discussion, a student asks: "Why does a piece of pitchblende keep emitting radiation day after day without ever running out?" Which answer is correct?
  1. The rock takes in energy from sunlight every day, which keeps it radioactive.
  2. Uranium-238 has a very long half-life (about 4.5 billion years), so only a tiny fraction of its nuclei decay each day and the activity stays almost constant.
  3. The rock only seems to emit radiation because the photographic film is slowly sending energy back.
  4. Radioactive decay is quickly refilled by new uranium nuclei that the rock makes from the air.
Correct answer: B
Half-life explains why the rock seems to never stop. With a half-life of billions of years, very few nuclei decay in any given day, so the radiation continues for an extremely long time with no outside energy input.
