Use after: Predict phase — Think-Pair-Share prediction sketch: what happens inside the heart during ONE beat
Q1. Before the cardiac cycle is taught, a learner is asked what happens inside the heart with each beat. Which prediction is correct?
  1. The heart sucks blood in like a sponge and the blood leaves by itself, without any squeezing
  2. The muscular walls of the heart squeeze to push blood out, and valves keep the blood moving in one direction
  3. The valves are the parts that pump the blood, while the heart muscle stays still
  4. The heart squeezes in all directions, so valves are not needed
Correct answer: B
The heart is a muscular pump. Its walls contract to push blood out, and valves open and close to stop backflow. The idea that the heart only sucks blood in is the misconception to investigate.
Use after: Observe phase — Activity 1: Pulse Rate Investigation (resting vs. post-exercise recovery)
Q2. A learner presses two fingers on the inside of her wrist and counts 38 pulse beats in 30 seconds. What is her heart rate in beats per minute (BPM)?
  1. 76 BPM
  2. 19 BPM
  3. 38 BPM
  4. 114 BPM
Correct answer: A
30 seconds is half a minute, so double the count: 38 × 2 = 76 BPM.
Use after: Observe phase — Activity 1: Pulse Rate Investigation (finger placement and post-exercise counts)
Q3. After 2 minutes of star jumps, a learner wants to measure her pulse correctly. Which method is best?
  1. Count the beats for any length of time and write down the number without doubling or timing
  2. Press lightly with two fingers on the radial pulse (wrist) or carotid pulse (neck) and count for a timed 30 seconds immediately after stopping
  3. Wait 10 minutes to rest so the pulse is easy to find, then count for 30 seconds
  4. Press hard with the thumb on the wrist, because the thumb gives the strongest pulse
Correct answer: B
Two fingers should be used because the thumb has its own pulse. The count must be timed, here 30 seconds then doubled, and taken immediately after exercise so the post-exercise rate is recorded.
Use after: Observe phase — Activity 2: Heart Sounds Simulation ('Lub-Dub')
Q4. The heart makes a 'lub-dub' sound. The 'lub' is the closing of the bicuspid and tricuspid valves at the start of ventricular systole. What causes the 'dub'?
  1. The opening of the bicuspid and tricuspid valves as the ventricles fill
  2. The heart muscle knocking against the ribs
  3. The closing of the aortic and pulmonary semi-lunar valves at the end of ventricular systole
  4. The atria contracting against the ventricles
Correct answer: C
The second sound is made when the semi-lunar valves in the aorta and pulmonary artery snap shut at the end of ventricular systole, stopping blood from flowing back into the ventricles.
Use after: Explain phase — Cardiac cycle timeline on the board: calculating beats per minute from a 0.8 s cycle
Q5. At rest, one complete cardiac cycle takes 0.8 seconds (atrial systole 0.1 s, ventricular systole 0.3 s, diastole 0.4 s). How many cardiac cycles (heartbeats) happen in one minute?
  1. 75 beats per minute
  2. 80 beats per minute
  3. 48 beats per minute
  4. 0.8 beats per minute
Correct answer: A
There are 60 seconds in a minute, so 60 ÷ 0.8 = 75 beats per minute.
Use after: Explain phase — Cardiac cycle timeline on the board: cycle length at Kipchoge's racing rate of 170 BPM
Q6. During a race, an athlete's heart beats 170 times per minute. About how long does one complete cardiac cycle last?
  1. About 2.8 seconds
  2. About 0.35 seconds
  3. About 0.8 seconds
  4. About 110 seconds
Correct answer: B
One minute is 60 seconds, so each cycle lasts 60 ÷ 170 ≈ 0.35 seconds. This is much shorter than the 0.8 s at rest.
Use after: Explain phase — Introduction of the SINO-ATRIAL NODE (SAN) and the conductor analogy
Q7. Which statement correctly describes the sino-atrial node (SAN)?
  1. It is a nerve in the brain that must send a signal before every single heartbeat
  2. It is a valve between the right atrium and right ventricle that makes the 'lub' sound
  3. It is a blood vessel that carries oxygenated blood from the lungs to the heart
  4. It is a patch of specialised muscle in the right atrium wall that generates its own electrical impulse and sets the rhythm of the heart
Correct answer: D
The SAN is the heart's pacemaker. It fires by itself, and the impulse spreads across the atria and then to the ventricles. Nerve signals and adrenaline only adjust its rate.
Use after: Explain phase — Cardiac output calculation: stroke volume for Kipchoge and an average student
Q8. Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume. A trained athlete has a resting heart rate of 50 beats per minute and a cardiac output of 5 litres (5000 mL) per minute. What is her stroke volume?
  1. 5050 mL per beat
  2. 10 mL per beat
  3. 250 mL per beat
  4. 100 mL per beat
Correct answer: D
Stroke volume = cardiac output ÷ heart rate = 5000 mL ÷ 50 = 100 mL per beat. A larger stroke volume means fewer beats are needed to deliver the same output.
Use after: Model Building phase — Model Narration, Sentence 3: why Kipchoge's heart recovers faster than ours
Q9. After exercise stops, a trained runner's heart rate falls quickly. Which explanation is best?
  1. A large stroke volume alone makes the heart rate drop quickly after exercise
  2. Nerve signals that slow the SAN take over again from adrenaline, and trained muscles use oxygen efficiently so less extra blood is needed
  3. The valves close permanently until the runner has rested
  4. The SAN stops firing for a few minutes so the heart can rest
Correct answer: B
Adrenaline speeds up the SAN during exercise. Once exercise stops, nerve signals slow the SAN again, and efficient trained muscles need less extra blood. Stroke volume explains the low resting rate but not how quickly the rate falls.
