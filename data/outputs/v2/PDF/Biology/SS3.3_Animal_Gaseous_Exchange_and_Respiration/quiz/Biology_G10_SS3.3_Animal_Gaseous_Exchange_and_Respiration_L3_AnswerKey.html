Q1. A student must pass oxygen across a surface as quickly as possible. She compares a smooth, flat, dry sheet of plastic with a crumpled, wet sponge. Which statement best explains why the crumpled, wet sponge is the better model of a gas-exchange surface?
  1. Surface area does not matter as long as the surface is thin
  2. The folds give a much larger surface area, and the water film lets oxygen dissolve before it diffuses across
  3. A dry surface lets oxygen pass through faster than a wet one
  4. The sponge is heavier, so oxygen is pushed through it faster
Correct answer: B
Folding increases the area available for diffusion, and oxygen must dissolve in a moist film before crossing a surface. The flat dry plastic has neither feature.
Use after: Observe phase — Station 3: Insect Tracheal System, tracing the path of oxygen from spiracle to muscle cell
Q2. In an insect, oxygen must reach the muscle cells. Which is the correct path that oxygen follows?
  1. Spiracle → tracheole → trachea → muscle cell
  2. Spiracle → blood capillary → trachea → muscle cell
  3. Spiracle → trachea → tracheole → muscle cell
  4. Mouth → lungs → blood → muscle cell
Correct answer: C
Air enters through the spiracle, moves along the larger tracheae, and then into the narrow tracheoles that reach the cells directly. Insects do not use blood capillaries for oxygen transport.
Use after: Explain phase — Adaptation 2: Thin Walls (Short Diffusion Distance), comparing the alveolus wall with a sheet of paper
Q3. The wall between the air in an alveolus and the blood in a capillary is about 0.2 micrometres thick. A sheet of paper is about 100 micrometres thick. How many times thinner is the alveolus wall than the paper?
  1. 50 times
  2. 20 times
  3. 5 000 times
  4. 500 times
Correct answer: D
Divide the paper thickness by the wall thickness: 100 ÷ 0.2 = 500, so the wall is 500 times thinner. This short distance lets oxygen diffuse across very quickly.
Use after: Explain phase — Adaptation 3: Moist Surface, discussion of what happens if the alveolus dried out
Q4. Why must the lining of the alveoli stay moist for gas exchange to work?
  1. Oxygen must dissolve in a film of water before it can diffuse across the membrane
  2. Water in the alveoli carries oxygen around the body
  3. Water makes the alveolus wall thicker so it does not tear
  4. Moisture warms the air so that it moves faster
Correct answer: A
Gases cross the respiratory membrane when dissolved in a thin film of water. A dry surface would slow or stop diffusion.
Use after: Explain phase — Adaptation 4: Rich Blood Supply, concentration gradient and the 'team of buckets' analogy
Q5. What is the main job of the dense network of blood capillaries around the alveoli in keeping gas exchange fast?
  1. It stores oxygen inside the alveolus until the body needs it
  2. It carries oxygen away continuously, so the concentration gradient stays steep
  3. It keeps the alveoli moist by releasing water into them
  4. It makes the alveolus wall thicker and stronger
Correct answer: B
Flowing blood removes oxygen from the exchange surface, so the oxygen concentration in the alveolus stays higher than in the blood. This keeps the gradient steep and diffusion fast.
Use after: DQB phase — DQB update: Answered Today, New Questions Raised and Model Connection (question mark after the arrow)
Q6. After studying how oxygen crosses the alveoli into the blood, a class asks which part of the driving question is still NOT answered: 'Why does my body work so hard to get oxygen to cells during exercise, and what happens inside the cells when there is not enough?' Which question is still unanswered?
  1. What happens to the oxygen once it is inside the muscle cells?
  2. How does oxygen dissolve in the moist lining of the alveolus?
  3. Why do the alveoli have a large surface area?
  4. Why are the alveolus walls thin?
Correct answer: A
The lesson explains how oxygen gets into the blood through the alveolus adaptations. What happens to oxygen inside the cells is left for later lessons on cellular respiration.
Use after: Model Building phase — Comparative Respiratory Surfaces three-panel model and 'Big Idea' sentence
Q7. Humans, tilapia and insects have very different respiratory surfaces. Why do all efficient respiratory surfaces share the same adaptations of large surface area, thin walls and a moist surface?
  1. Because all animals live in water
  2. Because large surface area and thin walls only matter for small animals
  3. Because every animal breathes through lungs
  4. Because all animals face the same problem of getting oxygen to their cells fast enough to release energy from food
Correct answer: D
Cells in all animals need oxygen to release energy from food, and diffusion is only fast enough with these features. This is the 'Big Idea' of the comparative model.
Use after: End of lesson — Exit task: explaining to a fisherman at Kisumu market why a tilapia with damaged gill lamellae would struggle
Q8. A fisher at Kisumu finds that a tilapia has damaged gill lamellae, with many of the thin plates broken off. Why would this fish struggle to survive?
  1. Its blood becomes too thick to flow through the gills
  2. It cannot swim, so water cannot reach its gills
  3. Fewer lamellae mean less surface area, so it absorbs less dissolved oxygen from the water
  4. Its gills will become too dry for gas exchange
Correct answer: C
The many thin lamellae give the gills a large surface area for taking oxygen from water, which holds little dissolved oxygen. Losing lamellae reduces the oxygen absorbed, so the fish cannot meet its needs.
