Use after: Predict phase — Predict: two-sentence prediction on how small molecules get from the intestine into the blood
Q1. In Lesson 8, enzymes broke the starch in ugali into glucose. Why must starch be broken down into glucose before the nutrient can pass from the small intestine into the blood?
  1. Glucose is sweet, and sweet substances can pass through the wall more easily
  2. Pressure from the muscles of the gut squeezes whole starch molecules through the wall
  3. Glucose molecules are small and soluble, so they can cross the thin intestine wall, but starch molecules are too large
  4. Starch is absorbed in the stomach, so digestion only matters for fats
Correct answer: C
Only small, soluble molecules can cross the thin wall of the intestine. Enzyme action turns large starch molecules into glucose, which is small enough to be absorbed.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: structured observation table on the ileum wall, villus and microvilli diagram
Q2. Which feature of the ileum wall gives it a very large surface area for absorbing nutrients?
  1. One large pouch that holds food for a long time
  2. Finger-like villi whose surface cells have tiny microvilli (brush border)
  3. A thick, smooth wall with no folds, so food slides along it quickly
  4. A thick layer of muscle that squeezes nutrients into the blood
Correct answer: B
Villi project into the lumen, and the epithelial cells covering them have microvilli. Together they greatly increase the surface area available for absorption.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: two routes (blood capillaries versus lacteal) for different nutrients
Q3. After fatty acids and glycerol cross the epithelial cells of a villus, where do they go next?
  1. Into the stomach, where they are stored
  2. Into the blood capillaries, then along the hepatic portal vein to the liver
  3. Into the central lacteal, then along the lymphatic system
  4. Straight back into the lumen of the intestine
Correct answer: C
Fatty acids and glycerol recombine into tiny fat droplets that enter the lacteal and travel in the lymph. Glucose and amino acids are the ones that enter the capillaries.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain Part A: absorption and assimilation, two-sentence distinction
Q4. Glucose from a meal of ugali crosses the wall of the small intestine and enters a blood capillary. Later, a muscle cell uses some of that glucose in cellular respiration to release energy. Which statement describes these two events correctly?
  1. Entering the blood is assimilation, and use in the muscle cell is absorption
  2. Both events are assimilation
  3. Entering the blood is absorption, and use in the muscle cell is assimilation
  4. Both events are absorption
Correct answer: C
Absorption is the movement of digested nutrients from the intestine into the blood or lymph. Assimilation is the use of those nutrients by body cells, for example in respiration.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain Part A: what moves glucose across the thin epithelial wall (diffusion and active transport)
Q5. Carrier proteins in the epithelial cell membrane pull glucose and amino acids into the cell using energy. Why is this active transport useful in the small intestine?
  1. It lets large food particles pass through the wall without being digested
  2. It allows the body to absorb glucose and amino acids even when their concentration in the gut is low
  3. It breaks starch and proteins into smaller molecules
  4. It only works when there is more glucose in the gut than in the blood
Correct answer: B
Diffusion only works from high to low concentration. Active transport uses energy, so nutrients can be absorbed even when the gut concentration is low.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain Part A: blue arrow annotation, capillaries to the hepatic portal vein
Q6. Glucose and amino acids enter the blood capillaries of a villus. Which vessel carries them away, and where does it take them first?
  1. The lymphatic system, to the lungs
  2. The lacteal, to the heart
  3. The hepatic portal vein, to the liver
  4. The hepatic portal vein, to the kidneys
Correct answer: C
Glucose and amino acids travel in the blood from the villus capillaries along the hepatic portal vein to the liver. The lacteal and lymph route is for fats.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain Part B: Case Study 1, kwashiorkor in coastal Kenya
Q7. A five-year-old child in Kilifi eats mainly ugali and cassava, with very little fish or beans. The child has a swollen belly, flaking skin and stunted growth, and is diagnosed with kwashiorkor. Which nutrient is lacking, and which locally available foods would help?
  1. Water; drinking more water would cure it
  2. Iron; sukuma wiki and liver
  3. Carbohydrate; more ugali and cassava
  4. Protein; beans, ndengu, omena and eggs
Correct answer: D
The diet is high in carbohydrate but very low in protein. Without enough amino acids the body cannot build and repair tissues, so the child is stunted and swollen. Beans, ndengu, omena and eggs supply protein.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain Part B: Case Study 2, iron-deficiency anaemia in adolescent girls in Nairobi
Q8. A teenage girl in Nairobi often skips meals and rarely eats dark leafy vegetables or meat. A test shows her haemoglobin is below the level used to define anaemia, and she feels tired and looks pale. What is the best explanation?
  1. She has too much iron, which blocks oxygen in the blood
  2. She lacks iron, so she makes too little haemoglobin and fewer red blood cells carry oxygen to her cells
  3. She lacks protein, so her villi cannot form
  4. Her stomach absorbs too much food, so her blood becomes thin
Correct answer: B
Iron is needed to make haemoglobin. With too little iron, less oxygen reaches the cells, causing tiredness and paleness. This is iron-deficiency anaemia.
