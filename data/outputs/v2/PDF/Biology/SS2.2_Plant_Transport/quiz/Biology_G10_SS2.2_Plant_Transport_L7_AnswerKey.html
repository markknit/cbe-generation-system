Q1. A farmer in Kibwezi has a maize plant 3 metres tall. Which statement best explains what pulls water from the soil to the top leaf?
  1. The roots push all the water up the stem, and this push is strongest at 2 pm.
  2. Capillarity alone lifts the water the full 3 metres, because it creeps up narrow tubes.
  3. Gravity pulls water upward through the xylem when the sun is hot.
  4. Evaporation of water from the leaves pulls the water column up the xylem.
Correct answer: D
Water evaporating from the leaves through the stomata (transpiration) lowers water potential in the leaf and pulls the water column upward. Capillarity lifts water well under a metre, and root pressure only supplements this.
Use after: Observe phase — Part: potometer investigation, calculating average rate (mm/min) for each condition
Q2. In a potometer investigation, a lamp was directed at the leaves of a shoot. The air bubble in the capillary tube moved 27 mm in 6 minutes. What was the water uptake rate?
  1. 4.5 mm/min
  2. 27 mm/min
  3. 162 mm/min
  4. 0.2 mm/min
Correct answer: A
Rate = distance ÷ time = 27 mm ÷ 6 min = 4.5 mm/min. Giving only the distance, or multiplying instead of dividing, are common errors.
Use after: Observe phase — Part: potometer investigation, Condition D (petroleum jelly on the leaves) and class bar graph
Q3. In a potometer investigation, a group covered both surfaces of all the leaves with petroleum jelly. The bubble moved much more slowly than in the control. What is the best explanation?
  1. The petroleum jelly increased root pressure, which pushed the bubble backwards.
  2. The petroleum jelly blocked the stomata, so less water was lost by transpiration and less water was pulled up the stem.
  3. The petroleum jelly made the xylem vessels wider, so the water slowed down.
  4. The petroleum jelly made the water molecules stop sticking together.
Correct answer: B
Blocked stomata reduce water loss from the leaf. This weakens the transpiration pull and so reduces the rate at which the stem takes up water.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: guided cohesion-tension diagram and the chain-of-students analogy
Q4. Which property of water molecules allows the pull from transpiration at the leaf to move the whole water column in the xylem, right down to the roots?
  1. Water molecules change into gas inside the xylem vessels.
  2. Water molecules stick together through hydrogen bonds (cohesion), so the column moves like a chain.
  3. Water molecules are pumped up by the xylem walls using energy.
  4. Water molecules repel each other, so they spread out along the xylem.
Correct answer: B
Cohesion, the sticking together of water molecules by hydrogen bonds, lets a pull at the top move the whole column. This is the cohesion-tension mechanism, like students holding hands in a line.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: teacher's brief explanation of root pressure and guttation
Q5. Which statement about root pressure is correct?
  1. It is the main force lifting water on a hot, windy afternoon when the stomata are wide open.
  2. It is a pulling force produced by evaporation at the leaf.
  3. It is a supplementary push: roots take up mineral ions, water enters by osmosis, and water can be forced upward, as seen in guttation.
  4. It is the force that lets capillarity lift water 3 metres.
Correct answer: C
Active uptake of ions lowers the water potential of root cells, so water enters by osmosis and can push water up, especially at night or in moist conditions. It supplements transpiration pull and does not replace it.
Use after: DQB phase — DQB: sticky note answering 'Why does the Kibwezi maize wilt even when the farmer has already watered the soil?'
Q6. A farmer in Kibwezi waters her maize in the morning, yet it wilts at 2 pm on a hot, windy afternoon. Which explanation is best?
  1. Water is lost by transpiration faster than the roots can absorb it, so the plant loses water faster than it gains it, even though the soil is wet.
  2. Water moves down the xylem in the afternoon, so the leaves get no water.
  3. The soil water has disappeared completely, so there is nothing for the roots to absorb.
  4. The roots push too much water into the plant, which makes the leaves collapse.
Correct answer: A
Heat and wind raise the transpiration rate, which strengthens the pull. If the rate of absorption cannot keep up with the rate of water loss, the plant wilts even when the soil contains water.
Use after: Model Building phase — Model: adding cohesion-tension arrows, transpiration label and root pressure label to the cumulative transport model
Q7. A student is adding the Lesson 7 'engine' to a plant transport model. Which addition is correct?
  1. A transpiration label at the root, because the pull starts where water enters the plant.
  2. Arrows in the xylem pointing downward from leaf to root, because water is pulled by gravity.
  3. A root pressure label showing it as the main driver of water movement on a hot afternoon.
  4. Arrows in the xylem pointing upward from root to leaf, with a label at the stomata saying that transpiration creates tension that pulls the water column up.
Correct answer: D
The pull starts at the top of the chain, at the leaf, where transpiration through the stomata creates tension. Cohesion-tension arrows therefore point upward, and root pressure is only supplementary.
