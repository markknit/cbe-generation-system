Use after: Observe phase — Part A: Root observation with hand lens (root hair drawing)
Q1. A student looks at a maize seedling root with a hand lens and sees many tiny root hairs. Why are root hairs useful to the maize plant?
  1. They increase the surface area of the root for absorbing water and mineral salts
  2. They store food for the seedling
  3. They protect the root from insects and disease
  4. They pump water up to the leaves
Correct answer: A
Each root hair is a long, thin extension of a root cell. Many root hairs greatly increase the surface area in contact with the soil, so more water and mineral salts can be absorbed.
Use after: Observe phase — Part B: Osmosis investigation with potato strips in tap water and concentrated salt solution
Q2. Two potato strips, each 5 cm long, are placed in a beaker of concentrated salt solution for 15 minutes. What is most likely to happen to them?
  1. They become shorter and floppy because water moves out of the potato cells into the salt solution
  2. They stay exactly the same length and firmness
  3. They become shorter only because the salt dissolves the potato
  4. They become longer and firmer because they take in salt
Correct answer: A
The salt solution has a lower water potential than the potato cells, so water leaves the cells by osmosis. The strip loses water, becomes flaccid and shorter.
Use after: Observe phase — Part B: Osmosis investigation with potato strips (teacher demonstrates cutting a strip and asks why all strips must be equal length)
Q3. In the potato-strip investigation, all the strips are cut to the same starting length (about 5 cm). Why is this done?
  1. So that the strips will all absorb the same amount of salt
  2. So that the strips will fit more easily into the beaker
  3. So that any change in length can be fairly compared, because the only difference is the water or salt solution
  4. So that the potato strips will not need to be measured again
Correct answer: C
Starting length is a controlled variable. Keeping it the same means that differences in final length are due to the liquid (tap water or salt solution) and not to the strips being different sizes.
Use after: Explain phase — Whole-class explanation: building the root cross-section diagram and drawing arrows for water movement
Q4. Which list shows the correct path taken by water as it enters a maize root from the soil?
  1. Xylem → endodermis → cortex → root hair cell
  2. Root hair cell → endodermis → cortex → xylem
  3. Cortex → root hair cell → xylem → endodermis
  4. Root hair cell → cortex → endodermis → xylem
Correct answer: D
Water enters the root hair cell from the soil, crosses the cortex, passes through the endodermis, and finally reaches the xylem in the centre of the root.
Use after: Explain phase — Whole-class explanation: mineral salts and active transport (where does the energy come from?)
Q5. The soil around a maize root has very few mineral salt ions, but the root hair cells contain many more of them. How do the mineral salts enter the root hair cells?
  1. By osmosis, in the same way as water
  2. By simple diffusion from the soil into the root hair cell, with no energy needed
  3. By active transport, using energy (ATP) from respiration in the root cells
  4. By being pulled in through the Casparian strip without passing through any cell
Correct answer: C
The salts move from low concentration (soil) to high concentration (cell), against the concentration gradient. This needs active transport, which uses ATP from respiration. Osmosis and diffusion only move substances down a gradient.
Use after: Explain phase — Whole-class explanation: Casparian strip in the endodermis (a checkpoint)
Q6. The endodermis of a root has a waxy band called the Casparian strip. What does it do?
  1. It absorbs water directly from the soil into the xylem
  2. It forces water to pass through living cells, so the plant can control what gets into the xylem
  3. It makes mineral salts move by osmosis instead of active transport
  4. It stores extra water for the plant during the dry season
Correct answer: B
The waxy Casparian strip blocks the gaps between endodermis cells. Water must therefore pass through the living cells, which acts as a checkpoint for what enters the xylem.
Use after: DQB phase — DQB update: 'Why does the Kibwezi farmer's maize wilt even when the soil has water?' (green sticky-note answers)
Q7. A farmer in Kibwezi waters her maize in the morning with very salty irrigation water. By afternoon the plants are wilting, even though the soil is wet. Which is the best explanation?
  1. The soil water has a higher water potential than the root hair cells, so water moves in too fast and the plant wilts
  2. The roots cannot use salty water because mineral salts only enter by osmosis
  3. The wet soil is blocking oxygen, so the root hairs have grown too long
  4. The soil water now has a lower water potential than the root hair cells, so water cannot enter by osmosis and may even leave the root
Correct answer: D
Salt lowers the water potential of the soil water. If it falls below that of the root hair cells, osmosis stops or reverses, so the plant loses water and wilts even though water is present, as the potato strip in salt solution showed.
