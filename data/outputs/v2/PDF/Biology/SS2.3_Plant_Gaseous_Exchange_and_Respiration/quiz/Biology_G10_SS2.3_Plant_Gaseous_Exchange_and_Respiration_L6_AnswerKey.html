Use after: Observe phase — Station A — Temperature Effect: three beakers at 10, 37 and 55 degrees C
Q1. A group tests how temperature affects yeast respiration using three bottles at 10 °C, 37 °C and 55 °C. Which change would make this a NOT fair test?
  1. Using bottles of the same size for every temperature
  2. Counting bubbles for 1 minute at each temperature
  3. Using 5 g of yeast in the 10 °C bottle but 10 g of yeast in the 37 °C bottle
  4. Using 100 mL of the same 10% sugar-cane juice solution in every bottle
Correct answer: C
In a fair test only the independent variable (temperature) changes. Different amounts of yeast would be a second variable that also changes the bubble count.
Use after: Observe phase — Teacher probing question: 'What does each bubble represent in terms of chemistry?'
Q2. In the yeast investigation, students counted CO2 bubbles coming out of a tube each minute. What does the number of bubbles per minute tell us?
  1. How much water the yeast has absorbed
  2. How much heat the sugar solution has lost
  3. How much oxygen the yeast has stored
  4. How fast the yeast is respiring, because CO2 is a product of respiration
Correct answer: D
CO2 is released when yeast breaks down glucose, so bubbles per minute is used as a proxy for the respiration rate.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: whole-class discussion of the temperature trend (Station A)
Q3. The yeast bottle kept in ice-cold water (10 °C) produced almost no bubbles. What is the best conclusion?
  1. Cold water makes yeast respire without producing CO2
  2. Respiration has stopped completely because cold kills yeast
  3. Respiration is happening very slowly because enzymes work slowly at low temperature
  4. Yeast does not respire in water
Correct answer: C
Few bubbles means a very slow rate, not zero. At low temperature molecules have less kinetic energy, so enzyme-substrate collisions are less frequent.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: Claim-Evidence-Reasoning sentences for temperature (3 and 24 bubbles per minute)
Q4. A group counted 3 bubbles per minute at 10 °C and 24 bubbles per minute at 37 °C. How many times faster was the bubble rate at 37 °C than at 10 °C?
  1. 21 times
  2. 27 times
  3. 8 times
  4. 72 times
Correct answer: C
Divide the higher rate by the lower rate: 24 ÷ 3 = 8, so the rate was 8 times faster.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: Reasoning sentence linking temperature to enzyme activity and denaturation
Q5. In the temperature investigation, the rate of respiration rises as the temperature goes up to about 37 °C. Why would the rate fall again if the yeast were kept at a much higher temperature, well above its enzymes' optimum?
  1. The enzymes become more active because they have more kinetic energy
  2. The sugar turns into oxygen at high temperature
  3. The enzymes lose their shape (denature) and can no longer break down glucose
  4. Yeast cells become stronger and need less glucose
Correct answer: C
Above the optimum temperature the enzymes denature, so substrate no longer fits the active site and the rate of glucose breakdown falls. It is not that heat makes yeast stronger.
Use after: Explain phase — Station B — Sugar Concentration Effect: 2%, 10% and undiluted sugar-cane juice (about 20%)
Q6. Yeast was placed in 2%, 10% and 20% sugar solutions at room temperature. The rate rises with sugar concentration, but would level off at very high concentrations. What is the best explanation for levelling off?
  1. All the enzyme active sites are already occupied (saturated), so extra glucose cannot be used faster
  2. The extra sugar changes into oxygen
  3. Enzymes are destroyed by sugar at every concentration
  4. The yeast stops being alive as soon as sugar is added
Correct answer: A
More substrate increases the rate until the enzymes are saturated. After that, adding more glucose cannot increase the rate.
Use after: Explain phase — Station C — Oxygen Availability Effect: shaken bottle versus oil-covered bottle
Q7. Two yeast bottles at 37 °C contain the same juice. One has air above the juice and the other has a layer of cooking oil that keeps air out. The oil-covered bottle still produces CO2 bubbles. Which statement is correct?
  1. The oil-covered bottle respires anaerobically, so it can release CO2 but makes only about 2 ATP per glucose
  2. More CO2 bubbles always means more ATP is made
  3. Bubbles in the oil-covered bottle must be oxygen coming from the oil
  4. The oil-covered bottle is respiring aerobically and makes 36 to 38 ATP per glucose
Correct answer: A
With oxygen excluded, yeast ferments glucose anaerobically. CO2 is still released but the ATP yield is about 2, compared with 36 to 38 for aerobic respiration, so CO2 amount does not show ATP yield.
Use after: Model Building phase — Explain: misconception 'Yeast is not a plant, so these results do not apply to sukuma wiki'
Q8. A student says: 'Yeast is not a plant, so our yeast results tell us nothing about sukuma wiki leaves.' What is the best reply?
  1. Yeast and kale cannot be compared because only animals respire
  2. Yeast is an animal cell, so its results are the same as kale
  3. Yeast is a plant, so the results apply exactly to kale
  4. Yeast is a single-celled fungus, so it is only a model. Plant, animal and fungal cells all respire, so it suggests what leaf cells do, but leaves still need to be tested
Correct answer: D
Yeast is a fungus and is used as a model. All these cells release energy by respiration, so the yeast results suggest what happens in leaf cells, but the leaf must still be tested.
