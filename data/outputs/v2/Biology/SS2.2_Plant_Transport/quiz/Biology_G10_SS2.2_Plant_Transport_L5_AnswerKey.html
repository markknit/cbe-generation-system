Use after: Observe phase — Step 1: cutting thin cross-sections of the bean or sunflower stem
Q1. Your group cuts a cross-section of a fresh bean stem to look at under a hand lens. Why must the section be cut as thinly as possible?
  1. So that the stain can change xylem into phloem
  2. So that light can pass through it and the tissues can be seen clearly
  3. So that the vascular bundles are removed and only the pith is seen
  4. So that the stem stays alive for longer after cutting
Correct answer: B
A thin section lets light pass through, so the stained regions and bundles can be seen and drawn. A thick section hides the detail.
Use after: Observe phase — Kenya context: cutting, staining and sketching the maize stem and comparing it with the bean stem
Q2. A group cuts and stains a bean stem and a maize stem. Which description of the vascular bundles is correct?
  1. Both stems have vascular bundles scattered through the stem
  2. The bean stem has bundles in a ring, and the maize stem has bundles scattered through the stem
  3. The bean stem has bundles scattered, and the maize stem has bundles in a ring
  4. Both stems have vascular bundles arranged in a ring
Correct answer: B
The dicot (bean or sunflower) stem has its bundles in a ring. The monocot (maize) stem has its bundles scattered.
Use after: Observe phase — Step 3: drawing the cross-section and separating 'What I See' from 'What I Think It Is'
Q3. A student looks at a stained stem section through a hand lens. Which statement is an OBSERVATION (what I see) and not an inference (what I think it is)?
  1. The patches are the highway that the Kibwezi maize needs
  2. The cells in the dark patches are dead
  3. The stained patches carry water up to the leaves
  4. The dark patches form a ring near the outside of the stem
Correct answer: D
Describing the colour and arrangement of patches is something you can see directly. The other statements explain what the patches are or what they do, so they are inferences.
Use after: Explain phase — Jigsaw expert Group A (xylem experts): structure, wall thickness, dead cells and role in water transport
Q4. Which description of xylem is correct?
  1. Dead, thick-walled tubes that carry water and mineral salts upward
  2. Dead, thin-walled cells that store food in the pith
  3. Living cells with companion cells that carry water downward
  4. Living cells with thin walls that carry sugars downward
Correct answer: A
Xylem is made of dead cells with thick walls, forming tubes that carry water and mineral salts upward from the roots. Living thin-walled tissue with sugar transport describes phloem.
Use after: Explain phase — Jigsaw expert Group B (phloem experts): sieve tubes, companion cells and role in sugar transport
Q5. A student says: 'Phloem is just another tube like xylem, so it also carries water up to the leaves.' Which statement correctly describes phloem?
  1. It supports the stem because its walls are very thick
  2. It is made of dead cells and carries water only upward
  3. It is made of living sieve tubes with companion cells and carries sugars made in the leaves, mainly downward
  4. It stores water in the cortex of the root
Correct answer: C
Phloem is living tissue made of sieve tubes and companion cells. It carries manufactured food (sugars) from the leaves to other parts of the plant, mainly downward.
Use after: Model Building phase — Adding the stem layer to the cumulative transport model: labelling xylem and phloem in a vascular bundle
Q6. In one vascular bundle of a bean stem cross-section, which arrangement of xylem and phloem is correct?
  1. Both are found only in the pith
  2. Xylem towards the centre (pith side) and phloem towards the outside (epidermis side)
  3. Xylem towards the outside (epidermis side) and phloem towards the centre (pith side)
  4. Xylem and phloem are mixed together with no pattern
Correct answer: B
In each bundle the xylem lies on the inner side, towards the pith, and the phloem lies on the outer side, towards the epidermis.
Use after: Explain phase — Jigsaw expert Group D and Connect-Back: what could break the highway in the Kibwezi maize?
Q7. A farmer in Kibwezi waters her maize in the morning, but the plants still wilt and some die. The soil is wet. Which explanation fits what you have learnt about stem structure?
  1. If the xylem in the stem is blocked, for example by air bubbles, or the roots are destroyed, water cannot reach the leaves even though the soil is wet
  2. The vascular bundles in maize are scattered, so they cannot carry any water
  3. Wet soil always gives enough water, so the wilting must be caused by the phloem carrying too much sugar down
  4. Xylem carries sugars to the roots, so watering the soil makes no difference
Correct answer: A
Water must travel up through the xylem to reach the leaves. If the xylem is blocked or the roots are damaged, the highway is broken and the plant wilts permanently even when the soil has water.
