Use after: Observe phase — Activity A: Girdling Evidence Analysis
Q1. A ring of bark, including the phloem, is cut all the way round the trunk of a mango tree (girdling). After some weeks the roots below the cut start to die. Which is the best explanation?
  1. The roots cannot get water because the bark carries water up from the soil
  2. The roots make their own sugar, and this stops when the bark is cut
  3. The roots are damaged by too much sunlight after the bark is removed
  4. The roots cannot get sugars made in the leaves because the phloem that carries them down has been cut
Correct answer: D
Phloem carries sucrose made in the leaves (source) down to the roots (sink). A complete ring cut blocks this, so the roots starve and die.
Use after: Observe phase — Activity A: Girdling Evidence Analysis (sucrose data table)
Q2. In a girdled bean plant, after 48 hours the sucrose concentration was 4.2% above the girdle and 0.3% below the girdle. What does this data show?
  1. Sugar travels down the plant in the bark (phloem) from the leaves, and the girdle stopped it at the cut
  2. Sugar cannot move through any part of a plant
  3. Sugar travels in the xylem along with water, and the girdle slowed the water down
  4. Sugar is made in the roots and travels upwards in the xylem
Correct answer: A
Sucrose built up above the cut and was low below it. This shows the tissue that was removed, the phloem, normally carries sugar downward from the leaves.
Use after: Observe phase — Activity B: Pressure-Flow Model Demonstration
Q3. In a model of the pressure-flow hypothesis, Beaker A holds concentrated sucrose solution and Beaker B holds dilute sucrose solution. Each is separated from a water reservoir by a semi-permeable membrane, and the two are joined by a glass tube. The red solution is seen to move along the tube from A towards B. Why does it move?
  1. The solution moves because the water in Beaker B evaporates
  2. Sucrose molecules are pulled through the tube by gravity
  3. The solution moves because the membrane pumps sucrose into the tube
  4. Water enters Beaker A by osmosis, which builds pressure that pushes the solution along the tube towards B
Correct answer: D
The concentrated solution in A draws in water by osmosis. This raises the pressure in A, and the higher pressure pushes the solution towards the lower-pressure end (B).
Use after: Explain phase — Part A: Direct Instruction Consolidation (pressure-flow steps)
Q4. In the pressure-flow hypothesis, what happens at a sink, such as a root cell or a maize cob, that helps keep sugars flowing towards it?
  1. Water is pulled up from the sink by transpiration, which pushes sugar away
  2. Sucrose is unloaded and used, and water leaves the phloem by osmosis, so the pressure falls
  3. Sucrose is loaded into the phloem and water enters by osmosis, so the pressure rises
  4. Sucrose is made by photosynthesis, which raises the pressure
Correct answer: B
At the sink, sucrose is unloaded and used and water leaves the sieve tube by osmosis, so pressure is lower than at the source. The pressure gradient drives mass flow from source to sink.
Use after: Explain phase — Part B: Compare and Contrast (xylem and phloem T-table)
Q5. Which statement correctly compares xylem and phloem in a maize plant?
  1. Xylem and phloem both carry water and sugar in both directions
  2. Xylem carries water and mineral salts from the roots to the leaves by transpiration pull, and phloem carries sucrose and amino acids from source to sink by pressure flow
  3. Xylem carries sucrose and amino acids from source to sink by pressure flow, and phloem carries water up by transpiration pull
  4. Xylem is made of living sieve tubes, and phloem is made of dead hollow vessels
Correct answer: B
Xylem moves water and mineral salts upwards, driven by transpiration pull (cohesion-tension), through dead vessels. Phloem moves sucrose and amino acids from source to sink by pressure flow, through living sieve tubes.
Use after: Explain phase — Part C: Connecting to Kibwezi (Why do some plants die while others recover?)
Q6. On a hot, dry afternoon in Kibwezi, two maize plants both wilt. By morning one has recovered but the other has died. Which explanation best fits the plant that did NOT recover?
  1. A severe, long water deficit made the tension in the xylem so large that air entered and broke the water columns, so water could no longer move up
  2. The roots had too much sugar from the leaves
  3. It was thirsty, and thirst alone is enough to kill a plant
  4. The phloem carried all the water down to the roots, so none reached the leaves
Correct answer: A
When water loss is severe and long, the tension in the xylem can break the water columns (cavitation) and damage cells. The plant then cannot move water upward even if it is watered again. Plants with unbroken columns recover when it cools in the evening.
Use after: Explain phase — Part C: Connecting to Kibwezi (Why does the sukuma wiki under the mango tree barely wilt?)
Q7. A sukuma wiki plot shaded by a mango tree barely wilts, while maize in full sun nearby wilts badly. Which is the best explanation?
  1. Shade makes the phloem carry water instead of sugar
  2. Shade makes the soil produce more water than the roots need
  3. Shade lowers light and temperature, so less water is lost by transpiration and the leaf cells keep their turgor
  4. Shade stops the roots from taking in any water
Correct answer: C
In shade, light and temperature are lower, so stomata lose less water by transpiration. The xylem tension stays lower and the cells stay turgid, so the plants do not wilt.
