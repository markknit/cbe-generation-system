Q1. In an investigation, groups place the same yeast–glucose suspension in water baths at 10 °C, 25 °C, 37 °C and 50 °C and count the bubbles of carbon dioxide produced each minute. What is the independent variable?
  1. The time for which bubbles are counted
  2. The volume of yeast–glucose suspension
  3. The temperature of the water bath
  4. The number of CO₂ bubbles counted per minute
Correct answer: C
The independent variable is the one the investigator deliberately changes. Here it is the water bath temperature. The bubble count is the dependent variable, and volume and time are controlled.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: groups set up boiling tubes with 10 mL of 5% glucose–yeast suspension
Q2. In the yeast experiment, only temperature is meant to change between groups. Which of these must be kept the SAME in every group so the test is fair?
  1. The number of bubbles counted
  2. The rate of CO₂ production
  3. The volume of the yeast–glucose suspension (10 mL)
  4. The temperature of the water bath
Correct answer: C
Control variables such as the volume and concentration of the suspension and the counting time must be kept constant. Temperature is the variable being changed, and bubbles or rate are the results being measured.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: each group records three readings and enters its mean in the class data table
Q3. A group at the 37 °C station counts CO₂ bubbles for three successive minutes and gets 18, 20 and 22 bubbles per minute. What mean value should the group enter in the class data table?
  1. 18 bubbles per minute
  2. 22 bubbles per minute
  3. 20 bubbles per minute
  4. 60 bubbles per minute
Correct answer: C
The mean is the total of the three readings divided by 3. (18 + 20 + 22) ÷ 3 = 60 ÷ 3 = 20 bubbles per minute.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: structured question 2 – Why does the rate fall sharply between 37 °C and 50 °C?
Q4. The class data shows that the yeast produces much less CO₂ at 50 °C than at 37 °C. What is the best explanation?
  1. The glucose was all used up in the first minute at 50 °C
  2. The enzymes at 50 °C are used up because they work too fast
  3. The enzymes that control respiration lose their shape (denature) at the high temperature and can no longer work properly
  4. The yeast respires faster at 50 °C but the CO₂ cannot escape
Correct answer: C
Respiration is controlled by enzymes, which are proteins. Above the optimum temperature they change shape (denature), so they stop working and the rate falls.
Use after: Model Building phase — Model: adding the temperature–rate curve ('enzyme-controlled; optimum ~37 °C') to the cellular respiration diagram
Q5. A learner plots the mean CO₂ production rate of yeast at 10 °C, 25 °C, 37 °C and 50 °C. Which description matches the pattern the class results should show?
  1. The rate stays the same at all four temperatures
  2. The rate rises steadily and is highest at 50 °C
  3. The rate is highest at 10 °C and falls as temperature rises
  4. The rate rises from 10 °C to a highest value at about 37 °C, then falls at 50 °C
Correct answer: D
Enzyme-controlled reactions speed up as temperature rises towards the optimum (about 37 °C). Above the optimum the enzymes denature and the rate drops, which gives a rise then a fall.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: structured question 3 – connecting the data to the warmth felt after step-ups
Q6. After 2 minutes of step-ups, a learner's skin feels warm. Which statement best explains why body temperature rises during exercise?
  1. Oxygen is a hot gas that warms the muscles when it arrives
  2. Breathing faster pushes warm air into the blood
  3. The enzymes in the muscles denature and give out heat
  4. Cellular respiration in the muscle cells releases heat as a by-product
Correct answer: D
Cellular respiration releases energy, and some of it escapes as heat. When muscles respire faster during exercise, more heat is released and body temperature rises.
Use after: DQB phase — DQB: 'Still Wondering' card about Iten athletes and enzyme denaturation
Q7. An athlete training in Iten gets very hot, and body temperature rises to about 39–40 °C. Human enzymes denature at about 45 °C and above. Why do the athlete's respiration enzymes not denature?
  1. Enzymes in athletes cannot denature
  2. Body temperature rises to 50 °C but the enzymes only denature in yeast
  3. Sweating and blood flow to the skin carry heat away, and the body temperature stays below the temperature at which enzymes denature
  4. The enzymes stop working during exercise so heat cannot harm them
Correct answer: C
Body temperature rises only a few degrees in hard exercise, which is below the denaturing temperature. Sweating and skin blood flow remove the extra heat.
