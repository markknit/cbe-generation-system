Use after: Predict phase — Predict phase: individual list of everything needed to explain why breathing rate doubled and pulse rose after the Lesson 1 step-up exercise
Q1. A learner's pulse rises from 72 to over 120 beats per minute and breathing rate doubles during 2 minutes of step-ups. Which explanation is the best one?
  1. Breathing and pulse rise because the lungs and heart become tired and work harder to keep going.
  2. The heart beats faster to push air into the lungs, and breathing faster only cools the body.
  3. The working muscles respire faster, so they need more oxygen and must lose more carbon dioxide. Faster breathing takes in oxygen and faster heartbeat carries it to the muscles.
  4. The muscles stop respiring during exercise, so the body breathes faster to restart them.
Correct answer: C
Exercising muscle cells respire faster, so oxygen demand and carbon dioxide production rise. The lungs supply oxygen and the faster-beating heart transports it to the muscles in the blood.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe phase: red-pen critique of the partially completed exemplar model (missing lactic acid pathway)
Q2. After step-ups, a learner's leg muscles feel a burning sensation even though the learner was breathing hard throughout. What is the best explanation?
  1. The muscles produced too much carbon dioxide, which is the same as lactic acid.
  2. Breathing hard dried out the muscles, causing the burning feeling.
  3. The muscle cells ran short of oxygen for their energy demand, so some glucose was broken down without oxygen to form lactic acid.
  4. Too much oxygen reached the muscles and burned the muscle tissue.
Correct answer: C
When oxygen supply cannot meet demand, muscle cells use anaerobic respiration, which produces lactic acid and causes the burning feeling. This is why an exemplar model without the anaerobic pathway cannot explain burning legs.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain phase: Zone A - Comparative Respiratory Surfaces (Fick's Law named in Zone A)
Q3. Fish gill lamellae, insect tracheoles and human alveoli all allow fast gas exchange. According to Fick's Law, which change to a respiratory surface would INCREASE the rate of diffusion of gases across it?
  1. Making the oxygen concentration equal on both sides of the surface
  2. Reducing the surface area of the surface
  3. Making the wall thicker for extra strength
  4. Making the surface wall thinner so the diffusion distance is shorter
Correct answer: D
Diffusion is faster with a large surface area, a short diffusion distance (thin wall) and a steep concentration gradient. Only a thinner wall improves exchange.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain phase: Zone B - Human Breathing Mechanics (muscle names, rib movement, pressure change)
Q4. Which set of events correctly describes inhalation in humans?
  1. The diaphragm relaxes and domes upward, the ribs move down and in, the chest volume decreases and the pressure inside rises.
  2. The diaphragm contracts and flattens, the ribs move down and in, the chest volume decreases and the pressure inside rises.
  3. The intercostal muscles relax, the ribs move up and out, the chest volume increases and the pressure inside rises above atmospheric pressure.
  4. The diaphragm contracts and flattens, the external intercostal muscles contract and move the ribs up and out, the chest volume increases and the pressure inside falls below atmospheric pressure.
Correct answer: D
Contraction of the diaphragm and external intercostal muscles increases thorax volume, lowering the pressure inside the lungs below the outside air pressure, so air flows in.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain phase: Zone C - Gas Exchange Pathway (alveolus to muscle mitochondrion including haemoglobin, blood vessels and heart)
Q5. Which sequence correctly shows the pathway of oxygen from the air in the alveolus to the cells of an exercising muscle?
  1. It dissolves in the blood plasma, goes to the heart where it is used up, and only carbon dioxide reaches the muscle.
  2. It travels from the alveolus through air tubes straight into the muscle cells, as in insects.
  3. It diffuses into a blood capillary, joins haemoglobin in red blood cells, is carried by the blood (through the heart) to the muscle, then diffuses into muscle cells and enters the mitochondria.
  4. It is carried by white blood cells to the muscle, where it is stored in the cytoplasm and used for protein making.
Correct answer: C
In humans oxygen crosses the alveolar wall into capillaries, binds haemoglobin, is pumped by the heart around the body and diffuses into muscle cells, where mitochondria use it in aerobic respiration. Air tubes carrying gas straight to cells is the insect system.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain phase: Zone D - Respiration Equations (aerobic and anaerobic side by side with ATP yield noted)
Q6. Which statement correctly shows aerobic respiration of glucose and compares its energy yield with anaerobic respiration in human muscle?
  1. Aerobic: glucose → lactic acid + a large amount of energy. Anaerobic: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + a small amount of energy.
  2. Aerobic: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + a large amount of energy. Anaerobic: glucose → lactic acid + a small amount of energy.
  3. Both release the same amount of energy, but anaerobic respiration needs oxygen and aerobic respiration does not.
  4. Aerobic: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + a small amount of energy. Anaerobic: glucose → lactic acid + a large amount of energy.
Correct answer: B
Aerobic respiration completely breaks down glucose using oxygen, giving CO2, water and much more ATP. Anaerobic respiration in animals stops at lactic acid and releases far less energy.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain phase: Zone D - one worked RQ calculation for a Kenyan food example (ugali, nyama choma or githeri)
Q7. A learner works out the respiratory quotient (RQ) for a Kenyan food example. In an experiment, 160 cm³ of carbon dioxide is produced while 200 cm³ of oxygen is used. What is the RQ?
  1. 1.0
  2. 0.4
  3. 0.8
  4. 1.25
Correct answer: C
RQ = volume of CO2 produced ÷ volume of O2 used = 160 ÷ 200 = 0.8. Inverting the ratio gives the common wrong answer 1.25.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain phase: Zone E - Factors Affecting Respiration Rate (oxygen availability with high-altitude Rift Valley example)
Q8. People living at sea level often find exercise harder when they first go to a high-altitude place in the Rift Valley. What is the best explanation?
  1. At high altitude the enzymes for respiration are denatured by the cold, so respiration stops completely.
  2. The air at high altitude has less oxygen available, so less oxygen reaches the muscle cells and aerobic respiration is limited.
  3. The air at high altitude has more carbon dioxide, which is used by muscle cells instead of oxygen.
  4. The lungs at high altitude cannot move air in and out because the diaphragm stops working.
Correct answer: B
Oxygen availability is a factor affecting respiration rate. At high altitude there is less oxygen in the air, so less is available for aerobic respiration and the body must breathe faster to cope.
Use after: DQB phase — DQB phase: closing the question 'Why did our mouths dry out - how does the body keep the alveolus moist?' (posted in Lesson 3)
Q9. Why do we often feel thirsty and have a dry mouth after exercise when we breathe hard through the mouth?
  1. The alveoli stop producing fluid when oxygen is scarce.
  2. The lungs produce extra carbon dioxide, which removes water from the mouth.
  3. Air passing through the airways picks up water vapour from the moist lining, so breathing hard loses more water from the body.
  4. Lactic acid made in the muscles dries out the saliva glands.
Correct answer: C
The airways and alveoli keep a thin film of fluid and the air is warmed and moistened as it passes. Hard breathing moves more air, so more water vapour is lost and we feel thirsty.
