Use after: Observe phase — Dataset A: CO₂ concentration vs. relative rate of photosynthesis (plotting and annotating the plateau)
Q1. In an experiment at constant light and 25 °C, the relative rate of photosynthesis was 4, 12, 20, 26 and 28 units at CO₂ concentrations of 0.01%, 0.04%, 0.08%, 0.16% and 0.32%. What does the change from 0.16% to 0.32% CO₂ tell you?
  1. The plant has stopped photosynthesising
  2. Enzymes have been denatured by the high CO₂
  3. CO₂ is still the main limiting factor, so the rate will keep rising steeply
  4. CO₂ is no longer the main limiting factor, so another factor such as light or temperature is now limiting
Correct answer: D
The rate rises by only 2 units (26 to 28) when CO₂ doubles, so the graph has levelled off into a plateau. This means CO₂ is no longer in shortest supply and another factor, such as light or temperature, now limits the rate.
Use after: Observe phase — Dataset B: Temperature vs. relative rate of photosynthesis (reading values from the graph)
Q2. At constant light and 0.04% CO₂, a plant's relative rate of photosynthesis is 17 units at 35 °C and 3 units at 45 °C. By how many units did the rate fall between 35 °C and 45 °C?
  1. 20 units
  2. 12 units
  3. 14 units
  4. 10 units
Correct answer: C
The fall is the rate at 35 °C minus the rate at 45 °C: 17 − 3 = 14 units. This sharp decline shows that very high temperature harms the process.
Use after: Observe phase — Dataset B: annotating the sharp drop ('enzymes denatured?')
Q3. In a temperature experiment with constant light and 0.04% CO₂, the rate of photosynthesis rises to a peak at about 35 °C and then drops sharply by 45 °C. What is the best explanation for the drop?
  1. The light intensity becomes weaker at high temperatures
  2. The enzymes that control photosynthesis are damaged (denatured) by the high temperature
  3. The CO₂ in the air runs out as the temperature rises
  4. The leaf makes more chlorophyll, which blocks the reactions
Correct answer: B
Photosynthesis depends on enzymes such as RuBisCO. Above the optimum temperature they lose their shape (denature), so the rate falls even though light and CO₂ are unchanged.
Use after: Explain phase — Step 2: Limiting Factor Mini-Lecture with CER (githeri analogy)
Q4. A teacher explains a limiting factor using a githeri recipe with plenty of beans but only a little maize. Which statement best defines a limiting factor in photosynthesis?
  1. The factor in shortest supply relative to what the plant needs at that moment, which sets the overall rate
  2. The factor present in the greatest amount, because it controls the plant
  3. The factor that the farmer can change most easily
  4. Light intensity, which is always the limiting factor
Correct answer: A
Like the maize in the githeri, the factor in shortest supply relative to the plant's needs sets the rate. Which factor limits can change as conditions change.
Use after: Explain phase — Step 2: CER discussion, Scenario B (same plant moved into deep shade)
Q5. A sukuma wiki plant is moved into deep shade under a mango tree. It still has 0.04% CO₂ and a temperature of 25 °C. Which factor is now most likely limiting its rate of photosynthesis?
  1. Light intensity, because the light reactions are short of energy
  2. Carbon dioxide, because 0.04% is a very small amount
  3. Oxygen, because the mango tree uses it
  4. Temperature, because shade is always too cold for enzymes
Correct answer: A
In deep shade, light is in shortest supply, so the light reactions run slowly and the overall rate is low. This helps explain why the shaded kale grew slowly and became pale.
Use after: Explain phase — Step 3: Agricultural Application (Naivasha greenhouse tomato scenario card)
Q6. Greenhouse tomato farmers in Naivasha pipe extra CO₂ into their polytunnels during the day. Under which condition would adding more CO₂ NOT increase the rate of photosynthesis?
  1. When CO₂ is the factor in shortest supply
  2. When the plants are in full sunlight at a suitable temperature
  3. When light is very low or the temperature is far from the optimum, so one of these is limiting instead
  4. When the greenhouse has a low CO₂ concentration of 0.04%
Correct answer: C
Extra CO₂ only helps when CO₂ is the limiting factor. If light or temperature is limiting, the rate stays low however much CO₂ is added, as the plateau on the CO₂ graph shows.
Use after: Model Building phase — Adding the 'Limiting Factors Layer' to the cumulative photosynthesis model
Q7. Which pairing of limiting factor and the part of photosynthesis where it mainly acts is correct?
  1. Light acts on the light reactions; CO₂ is used in the Calvin cycle; temperature affects enzyme activity in the stroma
  2. Temperature affects only chlorophyll; CO₂ affects only the stomata
  3. Temperature acts only on the light reactions; light acts only on the Calvin cycle
  4. CO₂ acts on the light reactions; light acts on enzyme activity in the stroma
Correct answer: A
Light drives the light reactions, CO₂ is fixed in the Calvin cycle, and temperature affects the enzymes that work in the stroma. These are the three annotations added in the 'Limiting Factors Layer'.
Use after: End of lesson — Exit ticket: Kericho tea farmer (3-2-1 exit ticket)
Q8. A farmer in Kericho grows tea in cool, misty conditions (low temperature, moderate light, normal CO₂). She adds a fertiliser that releases extra CO₂ in the soil. Will this greatly increase her photosynthesis rate?
  1. Yes, because more CO₂ always raises the rate
  2. Only at night, when the stomata are open
  3. No, because low temperature is probably the limiting factor, so extra CO₂ will not help much
  4. Yes, because the extra CO₂ will make the air warmer
Correct answer: C
In cool conditions, enzyme activity is slow, so temperature is the bottleneck. As the temperature graph shows, the rate stays low at low temperatures, and extra CO₂ cannot fix this.
