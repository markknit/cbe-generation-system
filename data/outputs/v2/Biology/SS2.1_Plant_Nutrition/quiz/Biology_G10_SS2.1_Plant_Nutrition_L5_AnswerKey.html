Use after: Observe phase — Teacher shows a labelled chloroplast (outer double membrane, grana made of stacked thylakoids, stroma)
Q1. In a chloroplast, where do the light-dependent reactions of photosynthesis take place?
  1. In the stroma, the fluid around the grana
  2. On the outer double membrane of the chloroplast
  3. In the cell wall next to the chloroplast
  4. On the thylakoid membranes inside the grana
Correct answer: D
Chlorophyll, the photosystems, the electron transport chain and ATP synthase are all built into the thylakoid membranes. The stroma is where the Calvin cycle happens. Stacking the thylakoids into grana gives a large surface area for chlorophyll.
Use after: Explain phase — Q1: where the oxygen on the Elodea data card comes from, and what happens if photolysis is blocked
Q2. An Elodea (water plant) in bright light releases bubbles of oxygen gas. Which statement correctly explains where this oxygen comes from?
  1. It comes from glucose being broken down in the stroma
  2. It comes from water molecules that are split (photolysis) at Photosystem II
  3. It comes from chlorophyll molecules that break apart in the light
  4. It comes from carbon dioxide that is split inside the chloroplast
Correct answer: B
In photolysis, 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O₂, so the oxygen comes from water. A chemical that blocks photolysis would stop oxygen release, so the bubbles would fall.
Use after: Explain phase — Re-examining the Elodea data card (lamp at 10 cm = 34, 20 cm = 26, 40 cm = 8 bubbles per minute) with the cause-and-effect chain
Q3. In an Elodea experiment, a lamp 10 cm from the plant gave 34 bubbles per minute. At 40 cm it gave only 8 bubbles per minute. Which explanation fits these results best?
  1. Further from the lamp, less light is absorbed by chlorophyll, so fewer electrons are excited, less water is split and less oxygen is released
  2. Further from the lamp, the plant makes more ATP, which uses up the oxygen
  3. Further from the lamp, the plant has less water, so it cannot make any oxygen
  4. Light intensity does not affect the light-dependent reactions, so the difference must be a mistake in counting
Correct answer: A
Less light means less chlorophyll excitation, less electron flow and less photolysis. That means less O₂ is released, so there are fewer bubbles.
Use after: Explain phase — Q2: sukuma wiki under the mango shade became pale and grew slowly
Q4. A kale plant grows in the shade of a mango tree for several weeks. It becomes pale and grows slowly, even though it has enough water and carbon dioxide. What is the best molecular explanation for the slow growth?
  1. Shade makes the plant release too much oxygen, which poisons the chloroplasts
  2. Shade gives the plant extra carbon dioxide, which blocks the electron transport chain
  3. Shade stops the roots from taking in water, so photolysis cannot start
  4. Less light means less chlorophyll excitation, so less ATP and NADPH are made and less food can be built in the next stage
Correct answer: D
Reduced light means less chlorophyll excitation. That gives less ATP and NADPH, which slows the Calvin cycle, so less sugar is made and growth is slow. The question states that water and CO₂ are available.
Use after: Explain phase — Q3: the sukuma wiki recovers overnight even without sunlight
Q5. A kale plant recovers from wilting overnight, when there is no sunlight. Which statement about its energy supply at night is correct?
  1. The light-dependent reactions stop in the dark, and the plant gets energy by breaking down sugars and starch made during the day
  2. The plant stores ATP and NADPH in the leaf all day and uses them at night
  3. The light-dependent reactions run faster at night because the leaf is cooler
  4. The light-dependent reactions carry on at night using ATP stored from the day
Correct answer: A
Light-dependent reactions need light, and ATP and NADPH are used straight away rather than stored. At night the plant gets energy from the sugars and starch it made earlier.
Use after: Observe phase — Sequencing the reaction cards on the thylakoid diagram (ETC pumps H⁺; H⁺ flows back through ATP synthase)
Q6. How is ATP made during the light-dependent reactions?
  1. Light energy directly joins ADP and Pi in the stroma
  2. Oxygen reacts with glucose to form ATP in the thylakoid
  3. H⁺ ions flow back through ATP synthase, and the energy released is used to join ADP and Pi
  4. Electrons enter ATP synthase and are changed into ATP
Correct answer: C
Electrons moving along the ETC release energy that pumps H⁺ ions into the thylakoid. The H⁺ ions then flow back through ATP synthase, which makes ATP from ADP and Pi (photophosphorylation).
Use after: Model Building phase — Adding the key 'INPUTS to this stage / OUTPUTS of this stage' to the cumulative photosynthesis model
Q7. A learner adds a key to her photosynthesis model to show what the light-dependent stage produces. Which list of OUTPUTS is correct?
  1. Carbon dioxide, ATP and glucose
  2. ATP, NADPH and oxygen
  3. Glucose, starch and carbon dioxide
  4. Water, ADP and NADP⁺
Correct answer: B
The outputs of the light-dependent reactions are ATP, NADPH and O₂. Water, ADP+Pi and NADP⁺ are inputs, and glucose is only made in the next stage (Calvin cycle).
