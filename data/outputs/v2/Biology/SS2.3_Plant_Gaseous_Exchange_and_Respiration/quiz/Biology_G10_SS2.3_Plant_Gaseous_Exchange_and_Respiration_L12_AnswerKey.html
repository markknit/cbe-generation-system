Use after: Observe phase — Model Finalisation Checklist, item 2: mechanism of stomatal opening/closing
Q1. In a kale leaf, how do guard cells open a stoma?
  1. The guard cells lose water and shrink, which pulls the stoma open
  2. Air pressure inside the leaf blows the guard cells apart
  3. Potassium ions move into the guard cells, water enters by osmosis, and the guard cells become turgid and bend apart
  4. The guard cells divide to make a gap between them
Correct answer: C
Ions (K⁺) enter the guard cells, water follows by osmosis, and the swollen (turgid) guard cells curve apart to open the stoma. Losing water makes them flaccid and the stoma closes.
Use after: Observe phase — Model Finalisation Checklist, item 4: pneumatophore in a waterlogged environment (Gazi Bay)
Q2. Mangrove trees at Gazi Bay in Kwale County grow in waterlogged mud that has very little oxygen. Why do they have pneumatophores (breathing roots) that stick up into the air?
  1. To make food by photosynthesis in the mud
  2. To absorb more salt from the sea water
  3. To release oxygen so the mud becomes dry
  4. To take in air so the underground roots can get oxygen for aerobic respiration
Correct answer: D
Roots in waterlogged mud cannot get enough oxygen from the soil. Pneumatophores have pores that let air in, so root cells can still respire aerobically and release energy.
Use after: Observe phase — Model Finalisation Checklist, item 3: lenticel on a woody stem with gas exchange arrows
Q3. A tree has a thick, woody stem covered with bark. Which structure lets gases pass between the air and the living cells under the bark?
  1. Lenticels, which are small pores in the bark
  2. Guard cells in the waxy cuticle of the bark
  3. Pneumatophores, which are found on every woody stem
  4. The cuticle, which allows oxygen to pass freely
Correct answer: A
Lenticels are small openings in the bark of woody stems that allow gas exchange. The cuticle is waxy and reduces water loss, and pneumatophores are found only on plants in waterlogged soils.
Use after: Observe phase — Model Finalisation Checklist, items 5 and 6: aerobic respiration pathway and anaerobic respiration pathway with ATP yield
Q4. A plant cell breaks down the same amount of glucose by aerobic respiration and by anaerobic respiration. Which statement is correct?
  1. Aerobic respiration releases much more ATP, because glucose is broken down completely using oxygen
  2. Anaerobic respiration releases much more ATP because it uses no oxygen
  3. Aerobic respiration releases no ATP and only makes heat
  4. Both release the same amount of ATP, but anaerobic respiration is faster
Correct answer: A
In aerobic respiration glucose is broken down completely (glycolysis, Krebs cycle, oxidative phosphorylation) and releases far more ATP. Anaerobic respiration only partly breaks it down and releases little ATP.
Use after: Observe phase — Model Finalisation Checklist, item 7: relationship between photosynthesis and respiration (net gas exchange)
Q5. A kale leaf is in the dark at night. Its cells respire but cannot photosynthesise. Which gas movement through the stomata is expected?
  1. Only water vapour moves, because gases cannot pass through stomata in the dark
  2. Carbon dioxide leaves the leaf and oxygen enters
  3. Oxygen leaves the leaf and carbon dioxide enters
  4. Oxygen and carbon dioxide both stop moving completely
Correct answer: B
In the dark there is no photosynthesis, so only respiration happens. Respiration uses oxygen and produces carbon dioxide, so oxygen enters and carbon dioxide leaves. In bright light photosynthesis can make more oxygen than respiration uses, so net oxygen leaves.
Use after: DQB phase — Final Explanation writing: component 4, why warm water could increase bubble production
Q6. Cut kale leaves in warm water make more bubbles than leaves in cold water. Which explanation is best supported by what the class learned?
  1. Warm water makes the guard cells decide to make new holes in the leaf
  2. Warm water stops respiration so gas is pushed out of the leaf
  3. Warm water speeds up enzymes in respiration, and also makes gas in the leaf expand and dissolve less in the water
  4. Warm water adds extra oxygen into the leaf
Correct answer: C
Respiration is controlled by enzymes, which work faster when warmer (as the yeast data showed). Warmth also makes gas in the leaf expand and dissolve less in water, so more bubbles are seen.
Use after: DQB phase — Final Explanation writing: component 3, what the bubbles probably contain and what was not tested
Q7. Mwalimu Njoroge's class saw bubbles coming from kale leaves in warm water. The class never tested what the bubbles contained. Which conclusion is correct?
  1. The bubbles are gas from the leaf's air spaces, probably including carbon dioxide from respiration and, in light, oxygen from photosynthesis, but this was not tested
  2. The bubbles are pure oxygen, because plants only make oxygen
  3. The bubbles are pure carbon dioxide, because the leaf is respiring
  4. The bubbles are nitrogen, because this is the main gas in air
Correct answer: A
The bubbles were not analysed, so we can only say what they probably contain. Leaf air spaces hold a mixture of gases that includes respiratory CO₂ and, in light, photosynthetic O₂.
Use after: DQB phase — Teacher synthesis after the two group readings: stomata as doors, guard cells as hinges, and where the picture breaks
Q8. In the class explanation, guard cells were compared to hinges that open and close the stomata like doors. Where does this picture break down?
  1. Guard cells make the bubbles directly by respiring
  2. Guard cells are made of dead cells, so they cannot open
  3. Guard cells open the stomata only when the leaf is under water
  4. Guard cells do not decide anything; they respond to things such as light, water supply and carbon dioxide level
Correct answer: D
Guard cells are living cells that change shape in response to light, water availability and CO₂. They do not make choices, so the idea that they 'decide' is only a simplification.
