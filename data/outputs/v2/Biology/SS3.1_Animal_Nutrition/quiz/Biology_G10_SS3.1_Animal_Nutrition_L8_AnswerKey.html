Q1. A learner mixes a little unchewed, plain ugali with water and adds two drops of iodine solution. Ugali is rich in starch. What colour should the mixture turn?
  1. Colourless
  2. Blue-black
  3. Green
  4. Brick-red
Correct answer: B
Iodine solution turns blue-black when starch is present, and unchewed ugali is mostly starch. Brick-red and green are Benedict's test colours for reducing sugar, not iodine colours.
Use after: Observe phase — Observe: Benedict's test on tubes A (unchewed) and B (chewed) in the hot-water bath
Q2. Chewed ugali and unchewed ugali are each mixed with water, heated with Benedict's reagent in a hot-water bath, and compared. The chewed sample turns green to orange, while the unchewed sample stays blue. What does this result show?
  1. Saliva made the ugali wet, which changes the colour of Benedict's reagent
  2. Chewing turned the sugar in ugali into starch
  3. Amylase in saliva broke some starch into reducing sugar during chewing
  4. Unchewed ugali contains no starch at all
Correct answer: C
Benedict's reagent turns green, yellow or orange when reducing sugar is present. The sugar appeared only after chewing, so amylase in saliva must have changed starch into sugar. Wetness alone does not give a positive test.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: guided question 1 on pepsin and nyama choma
Q3. A person has no pepsin in their stomach. They eat a meal of nyama choma (meat). Which food molecule will they have the most trouble digesting?
  1. Fat
  2. Starch
  3. Protein
  4. Reducing sugar
Correct answer: C
Pepsin is the stomach enzyme that digests protein, and meat is rich in protein. Starch is digested by amylase and fat by lipase.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: two-column reference card and guided question 2 on lipase and avocado
Q4. Avocado contains fat. Which statement correctly describes how the fat is digested?
  1. Amylase in the mouth breaks fat into glucose
  2. Lipase in the mouth breaks fat into amino acids
  3. Pepsin in the stomach breaks fat into fatty acids and glycerol
  4. Lipase in the small intestine breaks fat into fatty acids and glycerol
Correct answer: D
Lipase acts in the small intestine and splits fats into fatty acids and glycerol. Pepsin acts on proteins, amylase on starch, and fats do not form glucose or amino acids.
Use after: Explain phase — Explain: teacher addresses the misconception that enzymes are destroyed after one reaction
Q5. After amylase has broken down one starch molecule in the mouth, what happens to the amylase?
  1. It is used up and must be replaced for each starch molecule
  2. It turns into maltose
  3. It joins the sugar and leaves with it
  4. It is not used up and can break down many more starch molecules
Correct answer: D
Enzymes are biological catalysts, so they speed up reactions without being used up. One amylase molecule can digest many starch molecules.
Use after: Model Building phase — Model: annotating the Lesson 7 digestive-system diagram with enzyme names and reaction sites
Q6. Which choice correctly matches each enzyme to where it acts in the human digestive system?
  1. Amylase: mouth and small intestine; pepsin: stomach; lipase: small intestine
  2. Amylase: stomach; pepsin: mouth; lipase: small intestine
  3. Amylase: mouth and small intestine; pepsin: small intestine; lipase: mouth
  4. Amylase: small intestine only; pepsin: stomach; lipase: stomach
Correct answer: A
Amylase acts in the mouth and the small intestine, pepsin in the stomach, and lipase in the small intestine. The other choices place at least one enzyme in the wrong organ.
Use after: End of lesson — End of lesson: summary sentence on the annotated digestive-system model
Q7. Why is chemical digestion by enzymes essential before nutrients can be absorbed into the body?
  1. It converts large insoluble food molecules into small soluble molecules that can be absorbed
  2. It makes food hotter so that it dissolves faster
  3. It turns all food molecules into starch, which the body can absorb
  4. It removes water from food so the molecules become smaller
Correct answer: A
Starch, proteins and fats are large and insoluble. Enzymes break them into small soluble molecules such as sugars, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol, which can be absorbed.
