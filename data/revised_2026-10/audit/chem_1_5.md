# Known audit items: chem_1_5

## Lesson-sequence items (teacher review list)
- **[teaching choice]** Group VII reactivity explained by electron affinity (L4) vs electronegativity/oxidising power (L7); argon as end of an electron-gain trend (L4) — Electron affinity vs electronegativity/oxidising power are compatible framings for Group VII reactivity; argon remark is a loose preview.

## Final Explanation reviewer findings (unverified; some are false alarms)
- **Part 1(b) exemplar, Aluminium's ionisation energy dip**: The exemplar says aluminium's 3p electron is 'slightly further out and easier to remove', but the lesson sequence taught this dip as the 3p sub-shell electron only in a Lesson 5 summary. More importantly, the Part 1 prompt itself asks students to explain 'what other factor' stops aluminium reacting. This is acceptable, but the exemplar's explanation that Al 'must lose three electrons' is the only factor given, and the real reason (a protective oxide layer on aluminium) is never named. Aluminium's non-reactivity with cold water is mainly due to its oxide layer, so the exemplar's account is incomplete and misleading.
- **Part 1(a) exemplar vs Part 1 table and Lesson 6/7**: The exemplar says sodium and magnesium have 'two full inner shells (10 inner electrons)'. This is correct, but in Part 1(b) the exemplar then says the ionisation energy 'rises from 496 (Na) to 738 (Mg)' and calls reactivity a 'sharp decrease', while the table shows Al (578) lower than Mg. The Part 1 prompt asks students to describe a trend in ionisation energy as 'increase' across the period, yet the data show a non-monotonic pattern. The exemplar's statement that Al 'has a higher nuclear charge than sodium so its tendency to lose electrons is much lower' contradicts the data, since Al's first ionisation energy (578) is only slightly higher than Na's (496) and lower than Mg's. Ionisation energy alone does not explain the Mg > Al reactivity order.
- **Part 4(a) exemplar**: The exemplar says argon's value is 'about three times larger' than sodium's and justifies it with 3 × 496 = 1488. The actual ratio is 1521 / 496 ≈ 3.07, so 'about three times' is correct, but the prompt asks 'how many times larger' and the exemplar then says 'slightly more than' without stating the ratio. Lesson 5 states 'more than three times'. This is a minor mismatch in wording but the prompt contains the table and answer is trivially derived. No substantive error.
- **Part 2(b) exemplar and Lessons 2/7**: The exemplar states shielding is by '10 inner electrons in sodium, 18 in potassium'. Sodium (2,8,1) has 10 inner electrons, which is correct, but potassium (2,8,8,1) has 18 inner electrons, also correct. However, the lessons say Group I trends are due to 'additional shells increase shielding', and Part 2's prompt tells students to refer to 'the number of electron shells, atomic radius, shielding and ionisation energy', which gives away the structure of the answer.
- **Part 3 (d) exemplar and Lesson 4**: The exemplar claims chlorine is chosen over bromine and iodine because it is the 'strongest oxidising agent' with the 'highest electron affinity'. In fact fluorine-to-chlorine electron affinity is anomalous: chlorine has the highest electron affinity of all halogens, which is fine, but the lessons' explanation that reactivity decreases down the group 'because electron affinity decreases' is stated only qualitatively. Also the exemplar states that chlorine's atomic radius is smallest 'of the three', consistent with Lesson 4. No contradiction.
