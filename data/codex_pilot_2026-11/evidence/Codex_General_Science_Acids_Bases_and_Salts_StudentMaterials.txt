Acids Bases and Salts — Student
Materials
Grade 10 • OpenAI-only pilot • teacher review copy


G1 — A cupboard of different substances
Fictional school-store case: labelled vinegar is sour, a bicarbonate solution is mildly
basic, and one stored salt has become wet while washing-soda crystals have become
powdery. These descriptions are a puzzle, not an instruction to taste or touch. Predict
whether one explanation can account for all changes. During the unit, build a
“Substance–evidence–explanation–safe use” chart.


G2 — Indicator investigation
Materials per group: labelled wells with 1 mL each of food vinegar diluted 1: 4 with water,
dilute sodium bicarbonate solution prepared by the teacher (1 g in 100 mL water), and
distilled water; universal-indicator paper and its manufacturer’s chart; separate droppers;
goggles. Put one drop on separate indicator strips, compare with the chart and record an
approximate pH and classification. Never return a dropper to another sample. Repeat
one measurement. If strips are unavailable, analyse this explicitly illustrative evidence:
vinegar pH3, bicarbonate pH8, water pH7.


G3 — What an acid/base model explains
Evidence cards for reading, not experiments on bodies: A stomach acid supports
digestion, but excessive acid may be neutralised by a suitable antacid base. Carbon
dioxide made during respiration can dissolve in water and make it more acidic. Many
chlorine bleaches are alkaline, but their colour-removing action is oxidation of coloured
substances, not a general property of all bases. In a simple aqueous model acids
increase H+ (more fully hydrated as H3 O+) and soluble hydroxide bases supply OH−;
not every base is a soluble hydroxide. Match each process to a role and state one limit
of the model.


G4 — A base neutralises acid
Teacher demonstration after local risk assessment: use a tray, goggles, dropper, stirrer
and universal-indicator paper. Put 2 mL of the prepared 0.1 mol/L hydrochloric acid in a
small open well or beaker. Add prepared 0.1 mol/L sodium hydroxide in small drops,
stirring and testing on fresh indicator paper. Stop at approximately neutral indication,
then add a small excess to a separate matched trial. Learners record direction of pH
change. Learners do not prepare or handle concentrated reagents. Fallback qualitative
evidence card: initial acid pH1; near-balanced mixture pH7; excess alkali pH10
(illustrative, not measured).

G5 — Acid and a carbonate
Class microscale activity only after teacher approval: each pair has an open cup on a
tray with 2 mL diluted food vinegar, one small pre-measured pinch (about 0.1 g) sodium
bicarbonate, a spatula and goggles. Add the powder slowly; observe without capping the
container. Record bubbling. Never taste or test a gas with a flame. Alternative evidence:
bubbles form when the two named reagents mix. Use the information
“acid+carbonate→salt+water+carbon dioxide” to identify products; discuss why bubbles
alone cannot identify a gas.


G6 — Acid and a metal
Teacher-only microscale demonstration in a ventilated laboratory: put one small
magnesium-ribbon piece (about 2 mm) in an open well on a tray; add 2 drops of
prepared 1 mol/L hydrochloric acid. Teacher and observers wear goggles and keep a
safe viewing distance. Observe bubbles and the metal becoming smaller. No flame, gas
collection or sealed vessel. No nitric acid or other metal is substituted. If the teacher
cannot provide the setting and approved risk assessment, use the observation card
instead: magnesium gets smaller and a gas forms.


G7 — Three salts exchange water with air
Illustrative comparison, not real measured data: each labelled salt starts at 5.00 g under
its stated conditions, then is exposed to air. Salt A ends at 5.30 g and remains a solid;
Salt B ends at 6.20 g and forms a solution; Salt C is a hydrated salt; it ends at 4.20 g
and becomes powdery. For this comparison the only mass transfer is water. Calculate
mass change and classify each behaviour. What evidence would you need before
applying this explanation to an unknown powder?


G8 — Prepare a fair exposure comparison
Teacher demonstration or prepared samples: clearly labelled small samples of calcium
chloride and sodium carbonate decahydrate(washing soda), kept in separate trays out of
reach. Teacher prepares an exposed and a covered comparison of each in advance,
records time and room conditions, and photographs or weighs them. Learners use
observations supplied by the teacher; no handling or heating. Decide which comparisons
are fair and whether the evidence supports water gain or loss. If samples or balances
are unavailable, use G7 only and mark this as data interpretation.


G9 — Useful salts and responsible use
Read the application cards: food-grade sodium chloride is used in food; fertiliser salts
supply nutrients to crops; some medicines contain salts; washing soda is used in
laundry. Excess fertiliser nutrients reaching water can contribute to eutrophication.

G10 — Independent assessment evidence
All numbers are illustrative. Aqueous solutions P, Q, R have approximate indicator pH3,
8, 7 near room temperature. A5.00 g sample ends at 5.80 g and forms a solution after
exposure to air; another 5.00 g hydrated sample ends at 4.50 g and becomes powdery,
with water the only exchanged material. A laboratory record names dilute hydrochloric
acid and magnesium; another names hydrochloric acid and calcium carbonate. Use
these data with the unit’s reaction and safe-use models.


G11 — Printed source summaries for responsible use
Use this printed source card when digital access is unavailable. These are paraphrased
classroom notes, checked 12 November 2026, not complete copies of the source pages.
WHO explains that sodium is needed for normal body functions, while excess dietary
sodium is linked to higher blood pressure. Reading food labels and avoiding
unnecessary added salt are general information points, not a personal treatment or
intake target. The US Environmental Protection Agency explains that excess nitrogen
and phosphorus in water can increase algal growth and harm water ecosystems.
University of Bristol explains that hypochlorite bleach acts by oxidation; bleach mixed
with acids can release hazardous chlorine. Never mix cleaners. Sources: WHO, Sodium
reduction: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/sodium-reduction ; US EPA,
The problem: https://www.epa.gov/nutrientpollution/problem ; University of Bristol,
