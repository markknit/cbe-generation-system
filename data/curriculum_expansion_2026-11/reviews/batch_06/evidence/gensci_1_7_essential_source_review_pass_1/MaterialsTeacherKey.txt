Microorganisms — Materials Teacher Key
Grade 10 • OpenAI-only curriculum draft • teacher review copy
MI1 — Microorganism groups and observable evidence
Supplied text evidence, not photographs: A: baker's yeast is a single-celled fungus used to raise bread dough. B: Vibrio cholerae is a bacterium; some strains cause cholera. C: measles virus causes measles and reproduces using host cells. D: ringworm is an infection caused by fungi, despite its name. Identify the microorganism group for A–D and one effect on people. Audit this fictional poster: 'Every microorganism is a bacterium and always harmful.' State two corrections. These descriptions tell you the named groups; they show no measured cell size, culture or diagnosis. Source notes: WHO Cholera, https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/cholera; WHO Measles, https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/measles; CDC Ringworm Basics, https://www.cdc.gov/ringworm/about/index.html (checked14Nov2026).
Task: Classify each source-approved example as a fungus, bacterium or virus. For each, distinguish what the image/model shows from what it cannot show, and identify one way it can affect people.
Teacher key and preparation
A fungus/useful bread-making; B bacterium/cholera-causing strains; C virus/measles; D fungi/ringworm. Not every microorganism is a bacterium or harmful: yeast offers a counterexample. Text classification does not establish appearance, scale or a real person's infection. Viruses are acellular infectious agents, not small bacterial cells; no image is required or supplied.
Preparation/limits: Use teacher-previewed sources and age-appropriate examples. Fungi, bacteria and viruses are distinct groups; a picture alone does not establish that an organism causes a particular infection. Avoid diagnosing people or implying all members of a group are harmful.
MI2 — Helpful and harmful effects
Supplied examples: A bakery buys baker's yeast (a fungus) to raise dough; selected lactic acid bacteria are used in controlled food fermentation; bacteria and fungi decompose organic residues; unwanted microbial growth spoils stored food; pathogens can cause infection. Classify the groups explicitly named in the first three examples, then sort the five effects as useful/harmful/context-dependent. Explain one economic benefit and one cost; distinguish a general possibility from a measured local claim. No prices or local production totals are supplied. Reference for controlled fermentation: FAO, Fermented Fruits and Vegetables, chapter5, https://www.fao.org/4/x0560e/x0560e10.htm (selected process information only; checked14Nov2026).
Task: Sort supplied examples into useful, harmful or context-dependent effects of microorganisms on people. Explain one economic benefit and one cost, naming the evidence needed for each claim.
Teacher key and preparation
Yeast=fungus; lactic acid bacteria=bacteria; decomposers include both. Bread-making and controlled fermentation are useful examples; decomposition recycles material but unwanted decomposition of sale food causes loss. Spoilage/infection can cause harm. Bakery sales are a possible benefit; unsaleable food/replacement costs are possible losses. No local amount can be inferred. Fermentation by selected organisms under controlled conditions is not permission to eat unknown growth.
Preparation/limits: Examples may include food fermentation, decomposition and spoilage or infection. Not all microorganisms are pathogens; preserve distinctions between economic examples and measured local impacts.
MI3 — Transmission route matching
Four fictional records for route reasoning, not diagnoses: A: a confirmed ringworm source record describes direct skin contact with an infected person; B: a confirmed measles source record describes breathing shared indoor air with an infectious person; C: a cholera teaching record describes drinking water contaminated with Vibrio cholerae; D: a malaria extension record describes a bite from an infected female Anopheles mosquito carrying Plasmodium parasites. Match direct contact, respiratory/airborne, contaminated water and vector routes. Give one uncertainty for each if these were unverified real reports. Sources: CDC Ringworm Basics; WHO Measles; WHO Cholera; WHO Malaria https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/malaria. D extends the source's three named groups: malaria is caused by a parasite, not a bacterium, fungus or virus.
Task: For four teacher-approved fictional scenarios, match the stated infection and contact history to a possible transmission route (direct contact, respiratory particles, contaminated food/water or a vector). Identify one uncertainty in each scenario.
Teacher key and preparation
A direct skin contact; B airborne shared air; C contaminated water entering the digestive system; D mosquito vector carrying a parasite. In real reports the claimed infectious status, exposure timing, actual contamination or mosquito infection would require verification. A route possibility alone does not establish infection. Do not classify the vector itself as a microorganism or malaria as viral. The three source groups remain the core classification task.
Preparation/limits: Use medically accurate, reviewed examples; routes are organism-specific. A scenario does not establish diagnosis or causation. Do not ask learners to disclose personal illness or contacts.
MI4 — Infection categories and evidence
Reviewed infection/source pairs: ringworm—fungal—CDC Ringworm Basics (contact with infected people/animals or contaminated objects); cholera—bacterial—WHO Cholera (ingestion of contaminated water/food); measles—viral—WHO Measles (airborne spread). Classify the infection, identify its supporting source and propose one measure matched to its route. Audit this fictional post: 'Ringworm is caused by worms, measles is bacterial, and antibiotics cure every viral infection.' Correct each claim using the supplied categories. These are source summaries, not personal diagnostic criteria.
Task: Sort reviewed examples of fungal, bacterial and viral infections. For each example, state the source that supports the category and one prevention or control measure that matches its transmission route.
Teacher key and preparation
Ringworm=fungal, CDC; avoid shared contaminated towels/equipment and follow professional guidance. Cholera=bacterial, WHO; safer water/sanitation. Measles=viral, WHO; vaccination according to current local guidance; ventilation reduces shared-air exposure but is not a substitute for disease-specific guidance. Ringworm is not a worm infection; measles is not bacterial; antibiotics do not treat viruses. CDC antibiotic guidance: https://www.cdc.gov/antibiotic-use/about/. No learner treatment advice.
Preparation/limits: Use reliable public-health or curriculum sources selected by the teacher. Do not present antibiotics as treatment for viral infections; no learner diagnosis or treatment recommendation.
MI5 — Match prevention to transmission
Fictional prevention leaflet with plans to audit: P: possible shared-air measles exposure—only disinfect drinking cups; Q: possible contaminated-water cholera exposure—use verified safe water, protect it in clean covered storage and improve sanitation; R: ringworm linked to shared towels—avoid sharing personal towels and clean shared equipment appropriately. Match each plan to its route, decide whether it addresses the stated route, improve P and give a limit of Q/R. Use the supplied WHO/CDC source notes.
Task: Evaluate a set of fictional prevention plans. Match handwashing, safe food/water handling, ventilation or avoiding shared contaminated items to the stated route; reject measures that do not address the route and explain why.
Teacher key and preparation
P fails to address shared-air transmission; ventilated spaces and qualified disease-specific guidance, including recommended vaccination, address that route better. Q addresses waterborne risk but depends on verified water treatment and avoiding recontamination; storage alone cannot make unsafe water safe. R addresses contaminated-item contact but not every other exposure; cleaning requires an appropriate approved procedure. No measure is universal.
Preparation/limits: Measures are examples, not a complete medical protocol. Emphasise route-specific prevention, access and reputable guidance; no stigma or blame.
MI6 — Control and prevention are related
Fictional notices: A, before a food-service event: obtain verified safe water and protect it from contamination. B, after a possible water contamination alert: stop using the suspect source and follow the responsible water/public-health authority's instructions before reuse. C: 'One vaccination prevents every microorganism infection.' Compare A's prevention with B's response/control; explain their limits and correct C. Use WHO Cholera and WHO Measles source summaries; no real alert or individual treatment is implied.
Task: Compare a prevention action before exposure with a control action after an identified risk. Explain how sanitation, safe handling, isolation under professional guidance or vaccination where applicable can reduce risk, and name a limit of each example.
Teacher key and preparation
A reduces exposure before an event but safe handling must continue. B reduces further exposure after an identified risk; the notice does not identify the cause or establish when the source is safe again. Authority advice is required. Vaccines are disease-specific; a measles vaccine is not a vaccine against every infection. Prevention and control overlap; timing alone is not a universal scientific definition.
Preparation/limits: Keep vaccination/general health information aligned to current local public-health sources; do not give personal medical advice or imply one measure prevents every infection.
MI7 — Economic roles of microorganisms
Supplied economic evidence cards, all qualitative: E1, controlled starter bacteria transform food carbohydrates into acid during lactic fermentation, creating products people can sell (FAO chapter5 process summary). E2, bakery yeast produces gas during dough fermentation, allowing bread production (general biology example). E3, unwanted microbial growth may make stored food unsaleable, causing replacement costs (WHO Food safety, https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/food-safety). Explain two production benefits and one loss. Is 'our village lost half its income' supported? State what local records would be needed.
Task: Use supplied evidence cards to explain two ways microorganisms contribute to economic activity and one way microbial growth can cause loss. Separate a general example from a measured local claim.
Teacher key and preparation
E1 supports food-processing/product value, E2 supports bread production/livelihoods, E3 supports possible food loss and replacement expense. No local income fraction is supported. Sales, wastage, prices and defined time/place records would be needed. Useful fermentation is controlled; not all bacterial/fungal activity is desirable.
Preparation/limits: Possible examples include fermentation, decomposition, food spoilage and infection-related costs. Do not invent local prices or economic measurements.
MI8 — Food preservation choices
Provided comparison table in text: drying—less available water, but moisture can return; cooling—slows growth while cold storage is maintained, does not sterilise; heating—can reduce susceptible microorganisms with a validated process, does not prevent later contamination; salting/sugaring—sufficient concentrations lower available water, suitability and amounts are product-specific; controlled fermentation—selected organisms/process conditions may inhibit some spoilage microbes, unknown growth is not controlled fermentation. These are principles, not preparation instructions. Fictional case: a vendor has fresh leafy vegetables, dependable monitored refrigeration and no validated drying/salting/fermentation process. Compare all six methods and recommend a supported storage approach with a benefit and limit. CDC Food Safety https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/prevention/index.html supports chilling; the other mechanisms are supplied general science principles requiring product-specific validation before use. No tasting or food preparation.
Task: Compare drying, cooling, heating, salting, sugaring and fermentation using a provided information table. Recommend one method for a fictional food and explain the conditions, benefits and limitations using the supplied evidence.
Teacher key and preparation
Cooling is supported by the stated refrigeration; it can slow spoilage and reduce possible waste, but cold storage and safe handling must continue and no sample is certified safe. Drying changes the product and needs validated conditions; heating is a process step rather than protection from all later growth; salt/sugar suitability is product-specific; unknown fermentation is unsupported. Accept justified alternatives only if their required evidence/conditions are explicitly supplied, not invented.
Preparation/limits: Do not treat a classroom comparison as proof that food is safe. No tasting, consumption or home-preservation instruction; use teacher-reviewed food-safety sources.
MI9 — Plan a safe mould-growth investigation
Supplied paper-only plan review; do not set up a culture: Question—how might visible change in two fictional bread records differ under dry versus damp conditions? Intended comparison: same bread batch/size, container type, location, observation dates; moisture is the stated changed factor. Record external appearance on Day0/2/4/6 without opening a container; two samples alone would not provide replication. Proposed unsafe step to reject: 'On Day4 open and smell the spots.' Use the fictional MI10 log instead. Identify intended controls/change/schedule; reject the unsafe step; explain why a visible spot does not identify a fungus or bacterium. Name fungi and bacteria as possible spoilage groups from MI1/MI2, and explain one potential economic consequence of spoilage. Actual source project remains unperformed unless a separately approved, risk-assessed school protocol with competent supervision, containment and disposal is implemented; this paper plan is not that protocol. ASM safety guidance https://asm.org/guideline/asm-guidelines-for-biosafety-in-teaching-laborator.
Task: Review a teacher-prepared plan for observing mould growth on food substrates. Identify the comparison, observation schedule, records and safety controls. Use supplied dated images/data if no approved sealed observation is available; state clearly whether any growth was actually observed.
Teacher key and preparation
Moisture intended variable; bread/size/container/location/schedule intended controls; record external appearance only. Reject opening/smelling; use supplied records. Fungi/bacteria may spoil food but appearance cannot establish group/species or safety. Unsaleable bread/replacement cost is a possible consequence, not a measured local loss. Actual sample handling is not authorised by this paper task. Record project as not performed when only supplied data are used.
Preparation/limits: Any sample must be prepared and sealed by the teacher under school approval, allergy-aware material choice and a disposal plan. Learners do not open, touch, smell, incubate at body temperature or take samples home. A supplied record is not student performance evidence.
MI10 — Read observation records without overclaiming
Fictional dated log, not real experimental results: dry sample A and damp sample B are described as same batch/size/container/location, with no measured temperature or replicate samples. 2Nov2026 Day0: both no visible spots. 4Nov Day2: A no spots; B one pale patch. 6Nov Day4: A one small patch; B several patches. 8Nov Day6: A two patches; B larger overlapping patches. Compare changes and intended moisture condition; state two limits. Identify fungi/bacteria as possible food-spoilage groups using MI1/MI2, without identifying these patches. Explain one possible livelihood effect of food spoiled before sale. This supplied log cannot prove causation, species, growth rate, toxicity or food safety; no sample was handled by learners.
Task: Compare supplied dated observations from two sealed food-substrate samples. Describe the visible change, identify what was held constant or changed, and explain why the observations do not identify the microorganism or prove a general rule.
Teacher key and preparation
B has earlier/more extensive described patches than A in this fictional log; both change with time. Moisture is intended changed condition, other named factors intended controls. Unmeasured temperature and absent replication limit inference; no organism identity or causal general rule follows. Fungi/bacteria are candidate groups only. Unsaleable stock can reduce sales/increase replacement costs but no amount is given. No actual observation/performance credit for reading a fictional log.
Preparation/limits: Label all records as supplied unless genuinely observed. No organism identification from appearance alone and no opening of containers. Discuss uncontrolled conditions and replicate limits.
MI11 — Review a community food-preservation message
Fictional message to audit: 'Cooling kills every germ. Any food that looks normal is safe. A food-preservation poster proves our class informed the community.' Use MI8/MI10 and the supplied CDC Food Safety source note to correct all three claims. Draft a short message for a fictional vegetable vendor stating one preservation benefit, one limit, and a source. State what permission and actual delivery evidence would be needed before claiming community sensitisation. Optional approved digital task: open the named CDC page, locate its chill guidance and record title/date/claim; if print notes are used, mark digital use not observed.
Task: Draft a short, evidence-based food-preservation message for a fictional community audience. Check that it states a practical benefit and limitation, uses a reliable source and avoids claiming that one method prevents all spoilage or infection.
Teacher key and preparation
Cooling slows growth, does not kill every organism; appearance alone cannot establish safety; a draft is not delivery. Example: 'With reliable cold storage and safe handling, chilling vegetables can slow spoilage and reduce waste; it does not certify food safe. Source: CDC Preventing Food Poisoning.' Delivery requires school/community approval and an actual audience/date/record; digital evidence requires observed source use. Respectful discussion is task evidence only.
Preparation/limits: A learner draft is not a community sensitisation event. Actual distribution or presentation requires school/community approval and a reviewed message.
MI12 — Integrated infection and daily-life case
FINAL-ONLY HELD-OUT CASE. All events and business facts fictional; source summaries are paraphrases checked14Nov2026. A food-service record names Vibrio cholerae, a bacterium, and states that ingestion of contaminated water/food can transmit cholera, a bacterial infection (WHO Cholera, https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/cholera). A reported water contamination alert has not been independently investigated; no actual person is diagnosed here. Available risk actions: use an authority-verified safe water supply; keep that water in clean covered containers using clean utensils; follow current public-health instructions for the suspect supply. Covered storage does not treat contaminated water, and safe water can be recontaminated. Separate food-stock case: a vendor has fresh cut melon and reliable monitored refrigeration, but no validated drying, salting or fermentation process. CDC Preventing Food Poisoning https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/prevention/index.html lists cut melon among riskier foods and recommends appropriate chilling. Cooling slows microbial growth; it does not sterilise food or establish the safety of any particular batch. Fictional business record: spoiled stock cannot be sold and must be replaced; no quantities/prices are given. For the three final sections, cite which supplied source/fact supports each answer and identify what remains unknown. This case provides no proof of a real outbreak, consumption, testing, investigation or community action.
Task: For a fictional food-service case, identify the microorganism group and stated infection category, trace the supported transmission route, recommend route-matched prevention/control and food-preservation actions, and explain one economic effect and one evidence limit.
Teacher key and preparation
I1: V.cholerae=bacterium; cholera=bacterial; possible ingestion via contaminated water/food, supported by WHO summary; real exposure/contamination/cause still needs qualified investigation. I2: (1) authority-verified safe supply reduces ingestion risk, but verification and continued safe handling matter; (2) clean covered storage/utensils help avoid recontamination, but do not treat already unsafe water. Follow current authority instructions for suspect supply; neither measure guarantees safety. I3: monitored refrigeration is the supported choice for fresh cut melon under this case, supported by CDC summary; slowing growth can reduce possible spoilage/replacement costs. No exact savings or safety claim follows; time, temperature/history and actual product safety remain unverified. Distinguish WHO/CDC general summaries from invented events. No actual action/performance inference.
Preparation/limits: All case facts are fictional/supplied. Mark each inference against the stated evidence; do not diagnose a real person or claim that learners performed an investigation or community activity.
