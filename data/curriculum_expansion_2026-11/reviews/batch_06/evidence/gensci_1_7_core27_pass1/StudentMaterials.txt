Microorganisms — Student Materials
Grade 10 • OpenAI-only curriculum draft • teacher review copy
MI1 — Microorganism groups and observable evidence
Supplied text evidence, not photographs: A: baker's yeast is a single-celled fungus used to raise bread dough. B: Vibrio cholerae is a bacterium; some strains cause cholera. C: measles virus causes measles and reproduces using host cells. D: ringworm is an infection caused by fungi, despite its name. Identify the microorganism group for A–D and one effect on people. Audit this fictional poster: 'Every microorganism is a bacterium and always harmful.' State two corrections. These descriptions tell you the named groups; they show no measured cell size, culture or diagnosis. Source notes: WHO Cholera, https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/cholera; WHO Measles, https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/measles; CDC Ringworm Basics, https://www.cdc.gov/ringworm/about/index.html (checked14Nov2026).Task: Classify each source-approved example as a fungus, bacterium or virus. For each, distinguish what the image/model shows from what it cannot show, and identify one way it can affect people.
MI2 — Helpful and harmful effects
Supplied examples: A bakery buys baker's yeast (a fungus) to raise dough; selected lactic acid bacteria are used in controlled food fermentation; bacteria and fungi decompose organic residues; unwanted microbial growth spoils stored food; pathogens can cause infection. Classify the groups explicitly named in the first three examples, then sort the five effects as useful/harmful/context-dependent. Explain one economic benefit and one cost; distinguish a general possibility from a measured local claim. No prices or local production totals are supplied. Reference for controlled fermentation: FAO, Fermented Fruits and Vegetables, chapter5, https://www.fao.org/4/x0560e/x0560e10.htm (selected process information only; checked14Nov2026).Task: Sort supplied examples into useful, harmful or context-dependent effects of microorganisms on people. Explain one economic benefit and one cost, naming the evidence needed for each claim.
MI3 — Transmission route matching
Four fictional records for route reasoning, not diagnoses: A: a confirmed ringworm source record describes direct skin contact with an infected person; B: a confirmed measles source record describes breathing shared indoor air with an infectious person; C: a cholera teaching record describes drinking water contaminated with Vibrio cholerae; D: a malaria extension record describes a bite from an infected female Anopheles mosquito carrying Plasmodium parasites. Match direct contact, respiratory/airborne, contaminated water and vector routes. Give one uncertainty for each if these were unverified real reports. Sources: CDC Ringworm Basics; WHO Measles; WHO Cholera; WHO Malaria https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/malaria. D extends the source's three named groups: malaria is caused by a parasite, not a bacterium, fungus or virus.Task: For four teacher-approved fictional scenarios, match the stated infection and contact history to a possible transmission route (direct contact, respiratory particles, contaminated food/water or a vector). Identify one uncertainty in each scenario.
MI4 — Infection categories and evidence
Reviewed infection/source pairs: ringworm—fungal—CDC Ringworm Basics (contact with infected people/animals or contaminated objects); cholera—bacterial—WHO Cholera (ingestion of contaminated water/food); measles—viral—WHO Measles (airborne spread). Classify the infection, identify its supporting source and propose one measure matched to its route. Audit this fictional post: 'Ringworm is caused by worms, measles is bacterial, and antibiotics cure every viral infection.' Correct each claim using the supplied categories. These are source summaries, not personal diagnostic criteria.Task: Sort reviewed examples of fungal, bacterial and viral infections. For each example, state the source that supports the category and one prevention or control measure that matches its transmission route.
MI5 — Match prevention to transmission
Fictional prevention leaflet with plans to audit: P: possible shared-air measles exposure—only disinfect drinking cups; Q: possible contaminated-water cholera exposure—use verified safe water, protect it in clean covered storage and improve sanitation; R: ringworm linked to shared towels—avoid sharing personal towels and clean shared equipment appropriately. Match each plan to its route, decide whether it addresses the stated route, improve P and give a limit of Q/R. Use the supplied WHO/CDC source notes.Task: Evaluate a set of fictional prevention plans. Match handwashing, safe food/water handling, ventilation or avoiding shared contaminated items to the stated route; reject measures that do not address the route and explain why.
MI6 — Control and prevention are related
Fictional notices: A, before a food-service event: obtain verified safe water and protect it from contamination. B, after a possible water contamination alert: stop using the suspect source and follow the responsible water/public-health authority's instructions before reuse. C: 'One vaccination prevents every microorganism infection.' Compare A's prevention with B's response/control; explain their limits and correct C. Use WHO Cholera and WHO Measles source summaries; no real alert or individual treatment is implied.Task: Compare a prevention action before exposure with a control action after an identified risk. Explain how sanitation, safe handling, isolation under professional guidance or vaccination where applicable can reduce risk, and name a limit of each example.
MI7 — Economic roles of microorganisms
Supplied economic evidence cards, all qualitative: E1, controlled starter bacteria transform food carbohydrates into acid during lactic fermentation, creating products people can sell (FAO chapter5 process summary). E2, bakery yeast produces gas during dough fermentation, allowing bread production (general biology example). E3, unwanted microbial growth may make stored food unsaleable, causing replacement costs (WHO Food safety, https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/food-safety). Explain two production benefits and one loss. Is 'our village lost half its income' supported? State what local records would be needed.Task: Use supplied evidence cards to explain two ways microorganisms contribute to economic activity and one way microbial growth can cause loss. Separate a general example from a measured local claim.
MI8 — Food preservation choices
Provided comparison table in text: drying—less available water, but moisture can return; cooling—slows growth while cold storage is maintained, does not sterilise; heating—can reduce susceptible microorganisms with a validated process, does not prevent later contamination; salting/sugaring—sufficient concentrations lower available water, suitability and amounts are product-specific; controlled fermentation—selected organisms/process conditions may inhibit some spoilage microbes, unknown growth is not controlled fermentation. These are principles, not preparation instructions. Fictional case: a vendor has fresh leafy vegetables, dependable monitored refrigeration and no validated drying/salting/fermentation process. Compare all six methods and recommend a supported storage approach with a benefit and limit. CDC Food Safety https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/prevention/index.html supports chilling; the other mechanisms are supplied general science principles requiring product-specific validation before use. No tasting or food preparation.Task: Compare drying, cooling, heating, salting, sugaring and fermentation using a provided information table. Recommend one method for a fictional food and explain the conditions, benefits and limitations using the supplied evidence.
MI9 — Plan a safe mould-growth investigation
Supplied paper-only plan review; do not set up a culture: Question—how might visible change in two fictional bread records differ under dry versus damp conditions? Intended comparison: same bread batch/size, container type, location, observation dates; moisture is the stated changed factor. Record external appearance on Day0/2/4/6 without opening a container; two samples alone would not provide replication. Proposed unsafe step to reject: 'On Day4 open and smell the spots.' Use the fictional MI10 log instead. Identify intended controls/change/schedule; reject the unsafe step; explain why a visible spot does not identify a fungus or bacterium. Name fungi and bacteria as possible spoilage groups from MI1/MI2, and explain one potential economic consequence of spoilage. Actual source project remains unperformed unless a separately approved, risk-assessed school protocol with competent supervision, containment and disposal is implemented; this paper plan is not that protocol. ASM safety guidance https://asm.org/guideline/asm-guidelines-for-biosafety-in-teaching-laborator.Task: Review a teacher-prepared plan for observing mould growth on food substrates. Identify the comparison, observation schedule, records and safety controls. Use supplied dated images/data if no approved sealed observation is available; state clearly whether any growth was actually observed.
MI10 — Read observation records without overclaiming
Fictional dated log, not real experimental results: dry sample A and damp sample B are described as same batch/size/container/location, with no measured temperature or replicate samples. 2Nov2026 Day0: both no visible spots. 4Nov Day2: A no spots; B one pale patch. 6Nov Day4: A one small patch; B several patches. 8Nov Day6: A two patches; B larger overlapping patches. Compare changes and intended moisture condition; state two limits. Identify fungi/bacteria as possible food-spoilage groups using MI1/MI2, without identifying these patches. Explain one possible livelihood effect of food spoiled before sale. This supplied log cannot prove causation, species, growth rate, toxicity or food safety; no sample was handled by learners.Task: Compare supplied dated observations from two sealed food-substrate samples. Describe the visible change, identify what was held constant or changed, and explain why the observations do not identify the microorganism or prove a general rule.
MI11 — Review a community food-preservation message
Fictional message to audit: 'Cooling kills every germ. Any food that looks normal is safe. A food-preservation poster proves our class informed the community.' Use MI8/MI10 and the supplied CDC Food Safety source note to correct all three claims. Draft a short message for a fictional vegetable vendor stating one preservation benefit, one limit, and a source. State what permission and actual delivery evidence would be needed before claiming community sensitisation. Optional approved digital task: open the named CDC page, locate its chill guidance and record title/date/claim; if print notes are used, mark digital use not observed.Task: Draft a short, evidence-based food-preservation message for a fictional community audience. Check that it states a practical benefit and limitation, uses a reliable source and avoids claiming that one method prevents all spoilage or infection.
